# Council Testimony — Juicero brief

**Brief:** "Juicero — a $400 wifi-connected machine that presses proprietary juice packs sold on subscription."

**Gate 1: unverified.** This is a one-line product description, not a sourced customer problem. No named user, no job, no pain point, no data. Council voices below are commentary against a product description, not analysis against a validated brief. Proceeding per instruction — treat every observation as inference from the description alone.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen / Ulwick) — The brief names a product, not a job

- The brief states what the machine does (presses packs) and what it costs ($400 + subscription). It states no job. "Fresh juice, no cleanup, no chewing" is a plausible functional job [A], but the brief doesn't source it.
- If the job is "give me fresh-pressed juice with minimal effort," the fired incumbent is a countertop cold-press juicer (~$100–$300, no subscription) or a juice bar/delivery service (Suja, Pressed, local shops) — not "no juice at all." That's a meaningfully different competitive set than "convert non-juicers."
- The proprietary-pack + subscription model signals the real job Juicero is solving may be its own: recurring revenue capture, not customer convenience. When the mechanism (locked packs, wifi gate) exists to protect the business model rather than serve the stated job, JTBD calls that out directly — the job and the monetization are misaligned.
- Wifi connectivity for a juice press has no functional job attached in this brief. It reads as an emotional/social job ("smart home," "I bought the premium thing") bolted onto a functional appliance.

**Strongest objection:** This brief cannot support a JTBD read at all — there is no customer, no context of use, no stated alternative. Any job named above is invented to fill the gap, which is exactly what JTBD exists to prevent.

---

### Service Blueprint (Shostack / Bitner) — No frontstage/backstage exists in this brief to critique

- The brief describes a single frontstage action (press packs, drink juice) with zero visible backstage: pack manufacturing/distribution, subscription billing, wifi provisioning/firmware updates, and machine support/repair are all implied by "$400 wifi-connected machine... sold on subscription" but none are named.
- The wifi requirement is the sharpest blueprint signal in the brief: it's a backstage control point (DRM-style pack verification, per public reporting on the real Juicero) [CS: HIGH — this matches widely reported facts about the real Juicero product] disguised as a frontstage convenience feature. If that's the intent here, the brief should say so; it doesn't.
- No support process is named for machine failure, pack recall, subscription cancellation, or connectivity loss — all high-friction moments for a $400 hardware-plus-subscription product with no fallback path described (can the machine work offline? can packs be pressed by hand? [A] this is exactly the failure mode that ended the real product [CS: MEDIUM]).

**Strongest objection:** There's no backstage described at all — the brief is entirely frontstage marketing copy. A Service Blueprint pass against this brief is speculation dressed as framework output.

---

### Self-Determination Theory (Deci / Ryan) — Autonomy is the framework's flag on this brief

- Autonomy: a wifi-gated machine that requires proprietary packs actively removes user control over a basic daily action (can I press this juice, right now, without a network check?). This is the single sharpest SDT read available from the one-line brief.
- Competence: pressing a pre-portioned pack requires no skill and builds none — this is a "shortcut around competence" product, not a competence-building one. That's not inherently bad (not every product needs to build mastery) but it's worth naming since the brief markets a premium price against a near-zero-skill action.
- Relatedness: subscription model with no social/shared feature named — no relatedness dimension present in the brief at all, positive or negative.
- The trust model is inverted from what SDT wants to see: the machine doesn't trust the user to press their own juice without verifying a chip in the pack. That's the AI-systems-specific SDT question applied to hardware — does the trust model support or erode agency? Here it visibly erodes it, by brief design, not by accident.

**Strongest objection:** SDT is built for evaluating experience design, not restating "DRM annoys people" — the framework is being asked to do more interpretive work than a one-line brief can support without inventing user testimony that doesn't exist.

---

### Disruptive Innovation (Christensen) — This reads as sustaining-innovation-priced-as-disruption, and that's the tension

- $400 hardware + recurring subscription is a premium, sustaining-innovation price point aimed at existing juicers/wellness consumers who already buy cold-press juice — not a low-end disruption that creates a new market of non-juice-drinkers. Disruption theory would expect the disruptive version to be cheaper and simpler than incumbents, not more expensive.
- Nothing in the brief suggests a new market (people who don't currently make or buy juice) is being served — it looks positioned to compete with juice bars and premium juicers on convenience, which is sustaining, not disruptive, competition.
- If the "disruption" claim rests on the subscription/proprietary-pack model rather than the product itself, the loser is the countertop juicer manufacturer and the local juice bar — both lose share of a customer's routine, not market position from below.
- The incumbent (juice bars, high-end juicer brands) has little to defend here — a $400 wifi-gated machine is not an attacking-from-below threat; it's the kind of product an incumbent could shrug off, which itself is a disruption-theory red flag for the pitch.

**Strongest objection:** Nothing in this brief indicates disruption in the Christensen sense at all — it's a premium sustaining product, and calling it disruptive (if the brief owner intends to) would be a category error the framework should name plainly.

---

### Value Chain (Porter) — The chokepoint is the pack, and the brief tells you that already

- The proprietary pack is the deliberate chokepoint: hardware is very likely sold near cost or as a loss leader [A] and margin is captured on recurring pack sales — the classic razor/razor-blade value chain insertion. The brief's own structure (machine + proprietary packs + subscription) states this without needing inference.
- Margin effect: this model is designed to protect and extend margin over time (recurring, high-margin consumable) rather than create new value in the customer's chain — the customer isn't getting cheaper or better juice production, they're getting locked into a supplier relationship.
- The wifi requirement is a control mechanism over that chokepoint, not a value-chain insertion for the customer's benefit — it verifies pack authenticity to prevent supply substitution, which serves Juicero's margin, not the customer's value chain. [A] inferred from mechanism description, consistent with public reporting on the real product [CS: MEDIUM].
- Who controls the chokepoint: Juicero, fully — the customer has no substitution path (can't use third-party packs, can't skip the subscription and keep the machine useful in the brief as described). That's a maximally controlled chokepoint, which is good for the seller and a red flag for the buyer's negotiating position.

**Strongest objection:** Porter's framework assumes a value chain the customer is embedded in and extracting margin from; if this product's real function is capturing margin from the customer's grocery/beverage budget rather than inserting into a business value chain, "value chain" may be the wrong lens entirely — this may be better read as a consumer captive-market play than a value-chain insertion.

---

> **Disruptive Innovation and Value Chain diverge on what kind of business this is** — Disruptive Innovation reads this as a premium sustaining product that incumbents can ignore; Value Chain reads the proprietary-pack/subscription structure as a deliberately engineered, well-controlled margin-capture mechanism regardless of price tier. Those aren't contradictory exactly, but they point the brief owner toward two different worries: "will this win market share" (Disruptive Innovation says probably not, at this price/positioning) versus "is this business model exploitative of the chokepoint it controls" (Value Chain says yes, structurally). Route this to the brief owner — is the brief testing market viability or business-model ethics/durability?

> **Self-Determination Theory and Value Chain agree the wifi/pack-lock mechanism is the central design decision** but read it in opposite registers — SDT reads it as an autonomy violation against the user; Value Chain reads it as a rational, even well-executed, margin-protection strategy for the seller. This is not a gap to close. Both are true simultaneously and the brief owner has to decide which stakeholder's read the business is optimizing for.

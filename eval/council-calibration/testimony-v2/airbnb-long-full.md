# Council Testimony — "Air Bed & Breakfast" (airbnb-long.brief.md)

**Gate 1 status:** Sourced but thin. Problem statement carries `[R — founder-observed, n=1]` — real evidence, single instance, not fabricated, not broad research. Brief is honest about this in its own tags (`[?]` on the OKR key result, on the counter-metric, on generalization beyond scarcity-week conditions). Not empty, not unverified-and-hidden — proceeding on that basis, but every voice below is testifying against a founder-anecdote-grade evidence base, not validated demand.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — the brief smuggles the founder's job in as the customer's job

- The sourced job is narrow and specific: "I personally need to cover rent during a scarcity spike." That is a *host*-side, income-driven functional job, evidenced at n=1.
- The OKR ("strangers will book and stay in a stranger's home") extrapolates that into a *guest*-side job — trading hotel predictability/safety for price/availability during scarcity — with zero evidence this is the job travelers are hiring for. Gate 2 flags this itself: "No data yet on whether travelers will choose a stranger's home over a hotel when hotels aren't sold out." [A]
- What gets fired if this works: the hotel booking, or (more likely at this evidence stage) simply *not attending the event* / staying elsewhere. The brief doesn't distinguish "job replaces hotel" from "job replaces no-solution-at-all," and those are very different market sizes.
- Two-sided marketplace, two unexamined jobs: host's job (monetize idle space) and guest's job (find shelter under scarcity) are not the same job, and the brief treats "peer rental" as one undifferentiated bet.

**Strongest objection:** The evidence supports a host-side job (make rent) far better than it supports the guest-side job the whole business model depends on (strangers choosing to stay in strangers' homes). The brief is one anecdote away from confusing "we needed the money" with "they wanted this."

**Verdict:** Weak — job validated for one side of the marketplace, unvalidated for the other.
**Why this strength:** Founder n=1 evidence covers the host motivation directly; guest motivation is pure extrapolation, and the brief's own `[?]` tag on the OKR key result concedes this.

---

### Service Blueprint — stage-appropriate: absence of backstage design is expected at Gate 30, not a strike

- Applying the stage-appropriate rule: at `gate: 30` (pre-seed, founder-anecdote stage) there is no expectation that trust/safety, verification, or dispute-resolution backstage exist yet. Gate 3 saying "No trust/safety mechanism designed yet" is a correct, honest status report for this stage — not evidence of a bad brief.
- What *is* worth naming regardless of stage: the frontstage/backstage split here is unusually consequential. In most service blueprints, backstage failure degrades service quality. Here, backstage failure (no host verification, no guest screening) is a physical-safety failure inside someone's private home — a different risk category than a typical service gap.
- Support processes the brief doesn't address at all, stage notwithstanding: what happens mid-stay if something goes wrong (no emergency/dispute path sketched, even conceptually); who absorbs liability (Gate 3: "No liability model if a stay goes wrong" `[?]`).
- The counter-metric ("safety incidents ... must stay low enough not to trigger regulatory shutdown") proves the team already knows this is the load-bearing risk — it's just correctly deferred past Gate 30.

**Strongest objection:** Even granting the stage-appropriate pass on *design detail*, the blueprint gives no indication that trust/safety architecture is even the *named* next problem to solve — it's filed alongside regulatory strategy as one of three generic "what's missing" bullets, when it's arguably the single highest-leverage design problem in the entire concept.

**Verdict:** Stage-appropriate gap, not a weakness at this gate — but flagged as the top Gate-3 priority.
**Why this strength:** Scoring rule for gate:30 briefs holds backstage absence neutral; the objection is about prioritization *within* the "what's missing" list, not the absence itself.

---

### Self-Determination Theory — competence-support is the missing piece, and it's the one that matters most

- Autonomy: nominally high on both sides — host chooses to list, guest chooses to book. No coercion in the model as described.
- Competence: this is where it breaks. A guest has no information architecture (reviews, verification, host history) to competently judge "is this stranger's home safe for me." Gate 3 confirms none of that exists yet `[?]`. Autonomy without the information to exercise it well isn't really autonomy-supportive — it's autonomy plus risk.
- Relatedness: the implicit appeal of the model ("stay with a real person, not a faceless hotel") leans on relatedness — but the actual transaction as specced is anonymous and one-off, with no mechanism yet to build the interpersonal trust the relatedness narrative promises.
- Net: the psychological contract the concept is implicitly selling (community, connection) is ahead of the trust infrastructure that would make that contract safe to enter.

**Strongest objection:** SDT would say this model's appeal and its risk profile are both keyed to the same missing thing — trust/verification — so the emotional pitch ("like staying with a friend") is currently unsupported by anything that would let a guest *competently* believe it.

**Verdict:** Weak on competence-support specifically; autonomy nominally fine.
**Why this strength:** No reviews/verification/insurance exist per Gate 3 — competence to assess risk has zero scaffolding, and that's the dimension SDT weights most heavily for a stranger-in-home transaction.

### Disruptive Innovation — textbook non-consumption entry, but the undefended flank is regulatory, not product

- This reads as classic disruption-from-non-consumption: the target isn't "people who'd otherwise book a nice hotel," it's "people who currently have *no* option" during scarcity spikes — conference-week sellouts. Low-end/new-market disruption almost always starts here. [CS: HIGH — matches Christensen's canonical non-consumption pattern; not brief-specific data]
- Who loses position first: budget/mid-tier hotels during exactly their highest-margin weeks (scarcity pricing) — the brief's own origin story identifies the moment of maximum incumbent vulnerability.
- What the incumbent actually defends: not inventory, but regulatory standing, insurance frameworks, and safety certification — the precise things Gate 3 lists as absent here (`[?]` on liability, `[?]` on legal status per city).
- The brief names the counter-metric risk correctly ("regulatory shutdown") but "what's missing" only lists "regulatory strategy per major market" as a bullet, not a plan.

**Strongest objection:** Disruption theory predicts incumbents rarely fight on price — they fight on legitimacy and regulation, which is exactly the flank this brief has left completely open. The counter-metric names the threat; nothing in Gate 3 answers it.

**Verdict:** Moderate/plausible as a disruption pattern; the go-to-market flank (regulatory) is unaddressed.
**Why this strength:** The demand-side mechanism (non-consumption during scarcity) is a recognized, credible disruption entry point; the defense-side mechanism (regulatory response) has no brief content at all beyond a bullet point.

---

### Value Chain — the brief misidentifies the chokepoint it's actually fighting for

- Insertion point: between traveler and lodging supply, disintermediating the hotel's *inventory* function (a bed) while leaving its *trust* function (front desk, safety standards, liability coverage) entirely unaddressed per Gate 3.
- Margin effect: likely margin-creating for hosts (near-zero marginal cost supply — an already-owned spare room) and margin-compressing for hotels specifically at the scarcity-pricing moments that are their highest-margin windows — the exact moment the founders describe experiencing.
- Chokepoint: in a peer marketplace for strangers-in-homes, the durable chokepoint is trust infrastructure (verification, reviews, insurance) — not listing volume. Whoever locks that in first controls the category. The brief treats this as a to-do item under Gate 3, not as the central strategic question under Gate 2.
- Gate 2's stated bet ("idle housing supply is a viable substitute for hotel capacity broadly") is a supply-side framing; the real strategic bet, per value-chain logic, is "we build the trust layer before someone else does" — and that bet isn't named anywhere in the brief.

**Strongest objection:** The brief's Gate 2 bet is framed around supply substitution, but the actual defensible position in this value chain is trust infrastructure — and that's filed as an execution gap (Gate 3), not recognized as the strategic question the whole bet rests on.

**Verdict:** Weak strategic framing — correct market insertion point, wrong chokepoint identified as primary.
**Why this strength:** Supply-side framing (Gate 2) and unaddressed trust layer (Gate 3) are treated as sequential concerns in the brief; value-chain logic says they're the same question, and it's the one that decides who wins the category.

---

> **Named tension:** Service Blueprint and Value Chain diverge on how urgent the missing trust/safety layer is. Service Blueprint (applying the stage-appropriate rule) holds it neutral at Gate 30 — expected absence, not a defect. Value Chain treats the same absence as evidence the brief has misidentified its own strategic center of gravity. This is a decision for the brief owner: is trust infrastructure a Gate-3 build item, or is it the Gate-2 bet itself?

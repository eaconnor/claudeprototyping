# Council Testimony — New Coke brief
**Brief:** `eval/council-calibration/briefs/newcoke-short.brief.md` — one line, no citation: *"replace the original cola recipe entirely with a sweeter formula that wins blind taste tests."*
**Gate 1:** Unverified as written. The brief cites no customer research beyond an implied blind-taste-test result, no sourced problem statement, no population, no methodology. Historical facts about the actual 1985 New Coke case are well-documented externally [CS: HIGH] and are used below to ground testimony, but the brief itself supplies none of that grounding — it is a bare directive.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen / Ulwick) — Optimizing the wrong job entirely

- A blind taste test measures a narrow functional job: preference in a single anonymous sip, no context, no repeat exposure. It does not measure the job cola is actually hired for in real use — refreshment paired with identity, ritual, and habit (buying "my brand" at the machine, in the fridge, at the ballgame).
- The brief's framing ("wins blind taste tests") treats a lab proxy as if it were the customer's real hiring criteria. Sweeter formulas reliably win single-sip tests [CS: HIGH — documented pattern in beverage sensory testing] but that result does not transfer to sustained purchase behavior.
- "Replace entirely" fires the existing solution (original formula) without asking whether the customers currently hiring it for identity/social/nostalgic jobs were dissatisfied with it in the first place. Nothing in the brief indicates the *existing* customer base wanted a change — only that a competitive taste metric could be won.

**Strongest objection:** The brief conflates "wins a taste test" with "serves the job the customer hires the product for." Those are different jobs. A one-line brief that only measures the former should not authorize a total replacement of the latter.

---

### Service Blueprint (Shostack / Bitner) — No frontstage signal, no backstage contingency

- The brief describes a backstage decision (recipe reformulation, R&D taste-test process) with zero frontstage plan: no customer communication, no transition window, no coexistence period between old and new formula.
- "Replace entirely" is a full cutover with no rollback path named. There is no support process specified for the volume and intensity of customer response to a beloved product disappearing without warning [CS: HIGH — the real 1985 case required a dedicated customer hotline and reversal within ~3 months].
- The brief treats the blind taste test as the entire validation step. There's no blueprint line for what happens at the shelf, at the call center, or in retailer relationships the day the swap goes live.

**Strongest objection:** A brief authorizing total replacement of a flagship product contains no backstage-to-frontstage handoff plan and no contingency for reversal. That omission is not a detail — it's the whole operational risk of the decision.

---

### Self-Determination Theory (Deci / Ryan) — Autonomy removed, relatedness severed

- Autonomy: the brief's verb is "replace entirely" — customers are not offered a choice, not migrated, not opted in. The decision is made *for* them and *about* them, with no mechanism for voice.
- Relatedness: cola brands of this scale function as objects of personal and social identity (nostalgia, ritual, "the real thing") [CS: MEDIUM — well-attested in brand-identity literature, not sourced in this brief]. Removing the referent object without consent severs that relational anchor for the existing base, regardless of whether the new formula tests better.
- Competence is largely not in play here — this isn't a skill-based interaction — but the autonomy violation is severe enough on its own to predict backlash independent of taste quality.

**Strongest objection:** This brief is close to a textbook case of an autonomy violation: mass, unilateral, no-consent change to a product embedded in personal identity. The brief doesn't name this risk at all — it only names the taste-test win condition.

---

### Disruptive Innovation (Christensen) — Sustaining innovation, but on the competitor's terms

- This is sustaining innovation, not disruptive: the incumbent (Coca-Cola) is trying to build a *better* product for its *existing* customer base and against its established competitor, not creating a simpler product for a new or underserved market.
- Critically, "better" is defined here by the competitor's own performance metric — the blind taste test — a metric associated with Pepsi's public challenge positioning [CS: HIGH]. Chasing an incumbent-competitor's chosen metric on their terms is closer to imitation than genuine sustaining improvement.
- Who loses market position: paradoxically, if backlash occurs, it's the incumbent itself — its own installed base becomes available to be poached by the competitor whose metric was just validated as superior. The brief doesn't model this exposure.

**Strongest objection:** The framework would flag that this brief misapplies "improve the product for existing customers" logic to a category where the product's value is largely identity-based, not performance-based. Winning on a performance axis (sweetness) while destabilizing the identity axis is not a coherent sustaining-innovation move.

---

### Value Chain (Porter) — Wrong chokepoint identified

- The brief locates value in the recipe itself. In a mature, brand-driven beverage category, the actual chokepoints are brand equity and the bottler/distribution/shelf-space relationships — not the formula [CS: MEDIUM — standard value-chain read for CPG beverage, not sourced in brief].
- Margin effect of a recipe swap is trivial on the input-cost side (sweetener substitution costs are minor); the real margin exposure is downstream — volume loss, retailer confidence, bottler contract friction — none of which the brief addresses.
- The brief treats "wins blind taste tests" as if that were the value-creating lever. It isn't the lever that controls the category's margin structure; brand equity and distribution control are.

**Strongest objection:** This brief spends its entire content on the least strategically significant lever (recipe/taste) while saying nothing about the levers that actually control margin and market position (brand equity, bottler relationships, shelf control).

---

**Named divergence:**

> **Disruptive Innovation** and **Jobs-to-Be-Done** diverge on whether this even qualifies as "improvement." Disruptive Innovation, applied at face value, would classify this as *sustaining innovation* — a better product being built for the existing customer base via a measurable performance gain (taste-test win). Jobs-to-Be-Done disputes the premise that a taste-test win constitutes improvement *for that customer base at all*, because the metric doesn't correspond to the job the customer is actually hiring the product to do. This is a decision about which axis of "better" counts, not a gap in either framework — it belongs to the brief owner to resolve.

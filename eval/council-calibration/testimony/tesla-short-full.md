**Brief:** Tesla — "sell a $100k low-volume electric sports car to eventually fund a mass-market electric car." (`eval/council-calibration/briefs/tesla-short.brief.md`)

**Gate 1:** Unverified/empty. One sentence, no sourcing, no customer evidence, no `[R]`/`[D]` tags — this is a strategy assertion, not a sourced problem statement. Council below runs on named historical fact `[CS: HIGH]` plus framework inference `[A]`, not on brief-native evidence. Tier: MEAL.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — Conflates the company's job with the customer's job

- The brief states Tesla's job ("fund the mass-market car"). It does not state the *buyer's* job. Those are two different customers being treated as one line item.
- Functional job for the $100k buyer: outrun/out-spec a Porsche or Ferrari at that price point `[CS: HIGH — Roadster's stated competitive set at launch]`. Emotional/social job: early-adopter signaling — wealth plus technological foresight, without sacrificing performance status.
- Solution fired: traditional ICE sports cars, not other EVs — there was no EV competitive set in this price band at the time `[CS: MEDIUM]`.
- The brief's framing ("to eventually fund...") describes Tesla's motive, not why anyone buys the car. If marketing or product decisions get made off this sentence, they'll optimize for the wrong job.

**Strongest objection:** This brief has no customer in it at all — only a financing strategy wearing a customer-facing product as a costume. JTBD needs a buyer motivation to evaluate against; this brief doesn't supply one, it assumes it.

### Service Blueprint — Nothing to map; the brief has no backstage

- No distribution model named. No service/support model named for a low-volume niche vehicle — historically a real failure point (parts, repair centers) for this exact strategy `[CS: MEDIUM]`.
- No manufacturing/supply backstage named — batteries, drivetrain, chassis sourcing are the actual constraint on hitting "low-volume" at all, and the brief treats them as solved.
- The handoff between "buyer of sports car" and "funder of a future, unrelated mass-market product line" is a real frontstage/backstage seam — is the buyer told they're subsidizing R&D for a car they'll never own? The brief is silent on whether that's disclosed anywhere in the sales experience.
- There is no touchpoint, channel, or support process anywhere in this sentence to blueprint.

**Strongest objection:** This is the thinnest voice against this brief by design — a one-sentence strategy statement contains zero operational detail. Any blueprint testimony here is inference, not observation. Flag that explicitly rather than manufacture a service model that isn't in the brief.

### Self-Determination Theory — Reads as strong on relatedness if disclosed, silent if not

- Competence: a car that outperforms expectations for its category builds mastery/pride in ownership — that's a design strength of the underlying product, if true `[A]`.
- Relatedness: "help fund the future" is a powerful relatedness hook *if surfaced to the buyer* — joining a movement, not just buying a car. Tesla's actual 2006 messaging did this explicitly via public strategy communication `[CS: HIGH — the "Secret Master Plan" blog post]`. This brief doesn't say whether that disclosure is part of the plan or retrofitted after the fact.
- Autonomy: if the buyer doesn't know their purchase is financing a different future product, the "in control of my decision" feeling rests on an information asymmetry the brief doesn't resolve.
- Nothing here evaluates the actual driving/ownership experience — SDT has almost no surface to grip on a strategy sentence.

**Strongest objection:** SDT can only judge disclosure and experience design, and the brief supplies neither. Whatever SDT signal exists here was imported from outside knowledge of how Tesla actually communicated the plan — not from anything stated in this brief.

### Disruptive Innovation — This is not disruption by Christensen's own definition

- Christensen's model: disruption starts low-end or in non-consumption and moves up-market. This brief starts at the premium high end ($100k halo car) and intends to move *down* to mass market `[CS: HIGH]` — that's the inverse of the textbook pattern, sometimes called "new-market" disruption but genuinely contested as disruption at all.
- No incumbent loses market position at this stage. Porsche/Ferrari don't defend against a low-volume halo product; the actual threat to incumbents (GM, Toyota, dealer networks) only exists if and when the "eventually" resolves — which the brief doesn't specify how.
- What the brief calls a disruption strategy is really a capital-and-capability bridge. Naming it "disruptive" borrows credibility from a theory whose mechanics this plan doesn't follow.

**Strongest objection:** Applying this framework surfaces a category error in the brief itself, not just a gap — the strategy as stated inverts the disruption pattern it's implicitly claiming. That's a naming problem worth flagging to whoever wrote "eventually fund" as if it were self-evidently disruptive.

### Value Chain — "Eventually fund" is doing all the strategic work and none of it is shown

- The sports car inserts at the high-margin end of manufacturing/brand — using premium unit economics to fund capital-intensive capability-building (battery/drivetrain IP) rather than immediately contesting the mass-market chokepoints `[A]`.
- Margin effect: this isn't margin *insertion* into an existing chain, it's margin *accumulation* to build a parallel chain later. Different mechanism, different risk profile — no scaled-manufacturing or distribution chokepoint is named for stage two.
- The real chokepoints in the eventual mass-market chain — battery cell supply at scale, dealer/service network control — go completely unaddressed. "Eventually fund" assumes the leap from niche-car margin to mass-market-scale capital is a sizing problem, not a chain-control problem.

**Strongest objection:** The brief's entire second half — the actual strategic bet — is one unexamined word: "eventually." Value Chain analysis can't evaluate a transition it isn't given any mechanism for.

---

> **Jobs-to-Be-Done and Value Chain/Disruptive Innovation diverge on who the customer is.** JTBD reads the $100k buyer as an end-user solving a status/performance job, full stop. Value Chain and Disruptive Innovation read the same buyer as an instrument in a financing strategy — a means to a mass-market end the buyer may not know they're serving. Both are legitimate reads of the same sentence. This is a decision for the brief owner: is the buyer the customer, or the funding mechanism? The brief conflates the two; the frameworks don't agree on how to un-conflate it.

# Council Testimony — the Harness exec-deck product (Vertesia-OEM governance control plane)
Ran 2026-09-02 over `claims-harness-proposal-HP.md` + the re-baselined board (11 bets). Testimony, not synthesis. Contradictions preserved. Hole-sizes/gap-natures are Claude's deductive read against each lens's criteria (§18). Synthesis line for Beth: **[0% Claude — Beth synthesizes]**.

**Survival reads (all worse than the old product):** JTBD ~20% · Service Blueprint ~15% · SDT ~25% · Disruptive ~20% · **Value Chain ~10%**.

---

### 1. Jobs-to-Be-Done — sells one job (governance) the evidence says is unhireable; rescues it by inventing a second, untested job (resale)
- **#3 Governance-as-paid-product:** governance is a **hygiene job** (Kano must-be), not a hired one — 17/17 want control as a baseline (EV-AB11), Shawn "won't get someone to pay for it standalone." HP04's Derek quote is doing enormous load for one informant — and it's the same Derek who self-builds "in a day"; that's *tolerance for a wrapper*, not *demand for a plane*. **Framing error, not knowledge gap** — the data exists, it's misread. Resale rescue: partial, only in theory — it becomes a delighter for the MSP's P&L, but rests on ONE Jonathan quote who was never asked if *his* clients would pay *him*. Hole: **large**. Test: 5-10 discovery calls with Integrator/MIP MSPs — "would you resell AI governance to your clients, what would you charge" (not "would you buy Harness").
- **#5 Resale business:** a different job than the other ten — selling the MSP a GTM motion, not IT-ops efficiency. Nobody in n=8 or the survey was asked reseller-economics. **Knowledge-gap — never asked of anyone.** Hole: large.
- **#6 Pricing headroom:** the Good/Better/Best ladder describes increasing MSP *responsibility*, not increasing customer *value* — a cost-shifting ladder dressed as a value ladder. **Framing error.** Test: Van Westendorp that asks respondents to name the job Better/Best does that Good doesn't, before showing price.
- **#7 Real demand:** QBR wedge validated #1 but buyer-only (~93%, EV-AB13); small hole for the wedge, moderate for what stacks on it.
- **Riskiest:** #3 + #5 together — core paid job doesn't exist as stated; the escape hatch is one quote deep.
- **Missing bet:** what incumbent behavior gets *fired* when an MSP buys resale? MSPs already have informal governance (spreadsheet, Slack approvals, owner eyeballing the queue). Nobody asked what dies.
- **Survival: ~20%.** Kill: the resale-WTP test comes back like the standalone one did, and there's no third reframe left.

### 2. Service Blueprint — a three-layer backstage with the middle (Vertesia) erased by design, plus one seam building won't fix
- **#3:** frontstage sells "buy the plane" (control); backstage is "audit-after, no pre-screen" (EV-AB12) — the plane *watches, doesn't fly*. The promise and the mechanism describe two different products in the deck's own language. Resale makes it **worse** — the MSP stands frontstage before *their* client vouching for a control claim N-able's own evidence says isn't true yet, with less info than N-able has. **Evidence-contradiction, structural.** Test: run one real incident through audit-after — did it stop anything or only log it? — before any MSP repeats the claim downstream.
- **#5:** the MSP's client never sees "Vertesia" (branding stripped, HP06). When it breaks, whose support line? The MSP has none of N-able's diagnostic access into a system N-able *licenses, doesn't build*. Invisibility is the point, not a bug — **feasibility-wall** (visible-handoff vs white-label requirement collide). Hole: **large-to-fatal**.
- **#10 Failure path (home turf):** "empty beats wrong" only covers *missing data*; says nothing about the AI *acting wrong* in a client env. EV-WM04 (one MCP instance cascades across every coworker, no dependency tracking) + EV-WM05 (recovery flow "not yet drawn") = no designed recovery for the exact failure the resale tier is most exposed to. **Feasibility-wall.** Test: tabletop one MCP-down-mid-run scenario, write the recovery script; if none can be written today, that's the answer.
- **Riskiest:** #5 (invisibility by design) + #10 (undesigned recovery).
- **Missing bet:** Vertesia's own support/SLA capacity — 25 people, 12 customers; can they handle 300+ MSPs in Y1? Can N-able even diagnose a Vertesia-side failure it doesn't operate?
- **Survival: ~15%.** Kill: a real client-facing incident on the resale tier before recovery exists; the MSP eats the reputational damage for a system they don't control.

### 3. Self-Determination Theory — governance-as-hygiene is an autonomy-support *cost*; charging for it inverts the psychology
- **#3:** autonomy-support (13/17 fear rogue agents, EV-AX14) is a baseline need — you don't charge people to *not feel afraid*. HP19 half-admits it: "trust is THE buying criterion… never open with autonomous" — trust as *entry ticket*, not line item, which is what the research says and what the pricing contradicts. **Framing error compounding evidence-contradiction.** Resale rescue works only if it sells a genuinely NEW layer of reassurance (branded reporting, SLA, named Coworker) vs. the same hygiene relabeled — the deck doesn't distinguish. Test: show prospects the same capability framed as "baseline safety (free)" vs "a service you brand and resell" — do the same informants who called it table-stakes for themselves see it differently for their clients?
- **#9 Autonomy ladder:** MANUAL→ASSISTED→AUTONOMOUS admin-initiated (HP18) is textbook SDT-aligned — strongest positive item in the deck. But comprehension is SILENT ("all 8 described the pattern" proves they want a gate, not that they understand THIS gate). Test: the 10-second comprehension test, three mockups, cold.
- **#5:** relatedness risk — an MSP reselling white-labeled governance they can't inspect is a pass-through, not a competence-building act; may undermine their own professional-competence sense even as it grows revenue.
- **Riskiest:** #3 — not just unvalidated, psychologically *backwards*.
- **Missing bet:** whose autonomy at each tier? Good/Better = the MSP's control over their stack; Best = the MSP's *client's* control, mediated by an MSP reselling a capability built by a company neither has heard of. Different target person per tier, treated as one ladder.
- **Survival: ~25%.** Kill: an MSP's client discovers the "governance" is a monitoring log from a 25-person startup they were never told about; the trust cost lands on the reselling MSP, who fires N-able's platform to protect their own client.

### 4. Disruptive Innovation — the OEM is a sustaining catch-up; the resale tier is the only genuinely disruptive idea, and it's uncredited
- **#3 standalone:** a **sustaining** play — charge existing customers more for a commodity-hygiene feature; the classic margin-defense move that fails when incumbents (RMM/PSA who already touch these MSPs) add the same feature at zero marginal price (retention, not revenue). Resale **rescues it and here Disruptive diverges sharply**: bet #5, not #3, is the disruptive move — enabling resource-constrained MSPs to *supply* governance to their clients, attacking enterprise GRC/compliance vendors (Drata/Vanta-class) from below via a new value network. Textbook disruption structure; the deck defends #3 with disruption-flavored language ("buy the plane") while the actual logic lives one tier over in #5, uncredited. Test: find 5 enterprise-GRC-priced accounts an MSP's client can't afford; test whether Best-tier resale converts even one (the *nonconsumer* test).
- **#1 Vertesia:** OEM buys speed but the platform is "generic/horizontal" (HP08) — any competitor could license Vertesia-or-comparable and race the same MSP layer. Moat = execution speed + runbook IP, not the platform. **Feasibility-wall** — the differentiation this needs doesn't exist yet and isn't named as something to build.
- **#8 Above-the-stack:** clean disruptive positioning vs ScalePad (EV-AB16) — but the same zero-dependency move **cannibalizes N-able's own** N-central/Cove/Adlumin cross-sell. Framed purely as customer benefit; the internal channel-conflict is unnamed.
- **Riskiest:** #1 + #8 — OEM buys speed not defensibility; zero-dependency disrupts ScalePad externally *and* N-able internally, unmodeled.
- **Missing bet:** internal cannibalization — a bet built to NOT need the rest of N-able's stack gives MSPs a reason to shop that stack elsewhere. A disruption vector pointed inward, named nowhere.
- **Survival: ~20%.** Kill: a PSA/RMM incumbent licenses a comparable platform and ships an equivalent governance-resale motion before N-able's runbook IP is differentiated enough to matter.

### 5. Value Chain — the deepest chokepoint belongs to a 25-person startup on a 3-year term; the governance-moat theory doesn't survive that even if it's the right theory
- **#3:** governance is perceived as **overhead, not margin-additive** — you can't price a link the customer experiences as risk-mitigation-you-should-already-do. Resale is the **strongest rescue across all five lenses**: it repositions N-able as a wholesale layer and lets the MSP set retail price to their client (Shopify merchant-take-rate logic) — BUT only holds if N-able controls enough of the chokepoint to defend its cut, **and it doesn't.** Test: model the margin waterfall on one resold seat (Vertesia 2% pass-through + $1,500/MSP cost → N-able cut → MSP resale price) — does N-able's slice survive if Vertesia renegotiates at renewal?
- **#1 Vertesia — the chokepoint question, sharpest form:** Nicole's control-plane thesis says "own the plane that governs every agent," stack-independent (HP02). But the plane is **licensed from Vertesia** on a 3-yr term with a 2% inference pass-through (HP06/07) — the actual technical chokepoint (orchestration + inference) is owned by a 25-person, 12-customer startup, **not N-able**. Even if M5 resolves in governance's favor, N-able *still doesn't own the chokepoint it's betting the business on.* **Feasibility-wall — a structural fact of the deal, not a research gap.** Test: Vertesia customer-concentration + runway; does the 3-yr agreement have an exit/migration clause and IP escrow (diligence, not build).
- **#4 Data/interpretation:** the control-plane's actual value-add at the insertion point is thinner than the pitch — logging, not interpretation (EV-AB12). Large hole from a margin angle.
- **#2 MSP layer on a generic platform:** if the platform is licensable by anyone, the runbooks are the ONLY defensible-margin place — makes #2 far more load-bearing than its board weight suggests.
- **Riskiest:** **#1, unambiguously** — every other bet, including the #3-via-#5 rescue, is downstream of a chokepoint N-able doesn't own.
- **Missing bet:** the margin-transfer mechanics of the OEM as its own line — $450K/yr + 2% inference pass-through *in perpetuity* means Vertesia's take grows with N-able's success on a meter N-able doesn't set. "Does N-able's margin survive its own growth" is buried inside #1 as a partner-quality question when it's a margin-structure question.
- **Survival: ~10%.** Kill: Vertesia renegotiates or exits at the 3-yr mark (or is acquired/pivots first), and N-able finds the MSP layer it built has no chokepoint of its own to stand on.

## Disagreements (named, not resolved)
- **#5 resale — Disruptive vs Service Blueprint:** Disruptive reads it as sound low-end disruption (MSPs attack enterprise GRC from below); Service Blueprint reads the identical motion as structural oversell (MSP vouches frontstage for a backstage it doesn't control). A decision about which risk matters more.
- **#1 Vertesia — Value Chain vs Disruptive:** Value Chain treats the OEM as the fatal chokepoint under everything; Disruptive treats buy-vs-build as reasonable speed-to-market and locates the risk in #2 (MSP-layer differentiation). Route to whoever owns the partner negotiation.
- **#5 rescue — JTBD + Value Chain vs SDT + Service Blueprint:** two lenses say it rescues #3 (changes the buyer's *job* / N-able's *insertion point*); two say it introduces a *new* risk (relabeled hygiene / oversold trust transmitted downstream). Four lenses, two conclusions about the same three sentences in HP13.

## 01 · SO WE'RE MAKING QBRs.

It's our most-requested use case. #1 in the blind interviews. Top of the n=119 survey's drag-rank (2.90). Kenneth called cross-stack QBR "the holy grail" and had already paid real money for a tool that couldn't do it. There's a ticket: DEC-006 ships a "QBR Advisor" Coworker.

So we're making QBRs. Great. Making… what, exactly?

## 02 · WE THOUGHT IT WAS ABOUT THE DATA. IT'S ABOUT THE MEETING.

*(Vault taught us this move in 2018: "It's about data; not documents." Same lesson, new domain, inverted.)*

We walked in thinking the product was **data assembly** — pull eight sources, normalize, render a report. A month of research says the report is the *prop*. The product is the **ritual** — and the ritual has three jobs:

**Job 1 — growth.** Vanessa's first-ever EBR produced a referral and a new client. One UK partner closed a **£70,000 project** off the back of one. Stefanie's pitch for the whole practice is pipeline: "if you don't have a good lead-gen system in place, we don't want to discount the revenue that could be under our own noses."

**Job 2 — the heartbeat.** The named trigger for leaving an MSP: *"I never hear from my MSP."* Silence itself drives switching. The ritual is the pulse the client can hear.

**Job 3 — and this is the heavy one: liability management.** Listen to Stefanie's actual "why EBRs" speech — it's not a sales speech: *"MSPs have a lot on their shoulders. They are responsible for basically all of it. And if they're not communicating with their customer about the risk… if there's a cyber attack and the customer falls victim to a breach, who are they going to blame? They're going to blame the MSP. And the MSP can't say 'well, you didn't want to buy my security package' — because they never talked to them about the security package."* The meeting is where risk gets **named, priced, and formally handed to the person who owns the decision** — the client signs the risk register, every single time. The Cove story is this job working: a customer deferred a backup migration, signed the register saying so, quarter after quarter — then an attack went straight for the backups, and *"they had no one to be mad at."* The documented ritual converted a fireable disaster into a "you were right." And the job runs deeper than any one client: Stefanie's harder advice is portfolio-level — *"if there is a huge amount of risk, please drop them, please transfer them to somebody else. Don't keep that risk for yourself, because you're on the hook."* The EBR is how an MSP shapes a **safer book of business**, client by client, quarter by quarter — and per Joe, Lewis, and Julian, it's also what the insurance reality demands: documentation as claim *prerequisite*, sub-limits, whole categories of work MSPs now decline because the liability line is unclear. *(What the law actually says about the signed register — §BIG QUESTIONS. Spoiler: less than everyone thinks. The practice is real; the shield is untested.)*

And across all three jobs, the meeting is **not technical.** Three-hour EBR in London; the N-central guru sat ready the whole session. Nobody asked him one question. "We need to be speaking the love language of CEOs" — business outcomes, never graphs. Reports are "rear view mirror stuff"; the EBR is forward-looking, or it isn't an EBR.

The data matters — but it matters the way a caterer matters to a wedding. Nobody remembers the canapés. Everybody remembers whether the thing happened, and who signed what.

## 03 · IT NERDS KINDA HATE THIS STUFF.

Here's the human truth under the demand signal. Most MSPs are technical founders — "they didn't like their boss and ventured out." Their buyers are non-technical business owners. That disconnect is structural, and it's why the most valuable meeting in the account is the least favorite part of the job:

- **The curse of the expert** — too deep in the material to know what a lay executive needs. (A hired presentations coach's diagnosis in June; Stefanie's own first-person diagnosis in July, with her remedy attached: *"get over it… start with your easiest customers… practice."*)
- **It's very un-British to blow your own trumpet** — and both our primary sources are UK. The cultural register is real and flagged.
- **"They're not consultants."** Her words. The whole training exists because the conversation doesn't come naturally.
- **And nobody has time.** The excuse-trap ("client said no" taken at face value — "a terrible reason"). The prep is the roadblock, in her exact framing: *"if you don't have time to do EBRs, probably a lot of it is you don't have time to do the prep work."*

Now look at **our alpha persona** — the owner-operator, USER-001, "the buyer, escalation point, salesperson, and often still a technician." The person who'd have to *run* this ritual is the person with the least slack in the building. Our alpha almost certainly doesn't have time — which is exactly why the demand signal is so loud *and* why demand ≠ adoption.

And the counter-proof that it's doable: **Vanessa Chart** — deliberately non-technical Head of Client Delivery, runs EBRs solo start to finish, feeds the EOS scorecard, converts action items to tickets. It works. It also costs **two scarce people per cycle** (a technician gets pulled off the paying service desk to build the risk review) and tops out at a hard ceiling: *"four EBRs a week was actually not sustainable."*

One spec problem before we move on *(DIV-002)*: **Vanessa fits no actor in FSN-83143.** The buyer has a slot in our data model. The person who runs the meeting doesn't.

## 04 · HMW MAKE A TIME-CONSUMING THING LESS TIME-CONSUMING.

That's the design question, and it's the right door. One July nuance to carry through it: when the framework's author got a magic wand, **only one of her four wishes saved time.** The others — insights ("what it means, the impact, the consequences"), talking points in the love language, red/yellow/green grades — transfer *confidence*. Time is what the buyer pays for. Confidence may be what the operator actually hires.

And confidence after 15 minutes of prep doesn't come from the 15 minutes — the hours used to smuggle in familiarity, so the kit has to re-supply it deliberately. Four mechanisms, each corpus-anchored:

1. **Continuity** — the account state never goes cold (Stefanie ran 100 accounts with nerve because her living documents held the memory; the 15 minutes is a *delta review*, not a cram).
2. **Defensibility** — walking in knowing which claims survive challenge ("everything I'll say is Measured, or I'll say so"). The grades and tiers are armor, not decoration.
3. **Anticipation** — the kit knows the account's gaps, so it can pre-ask the five hardest questions this CFO might raise, honest answers attached. Curse-of-the-expert, inverted into a feature. Plus a rehearsal mode — the agent as skeptical CFO — which is just Stefanie's own practice habit ("do you mind me practicing on you?") on demand `[A — design hypothesis]`.
4. **Reps that fade** — scripting fully visible early, receding as the operator's own notes take over. Her pedagogy, built in: borrowed confidence must convert to owned competence, or we've built dependency (the W4 trap).

Hold both. Design for the hours; measure whether the value lands in the minutes or in the nerve.

## 05 · THE EBR'S MOST IMPORTANT PIECES.

We hold the actual artifact — Stefanie's own 15-tab template `[CS: VERIFIED — primary source]` — so this isn't a guess:

**The risk register is the center of gravity — because it's where all three jobs of the meeting converge.** A structured table (Threat, Level, Existing Controls, Treatment Plan, Residual Risk, Owner) that is simultaneously: the conversation agenda, the pipeline source (every unaccepted risk is a future proposal), and the liability instrument — clients **sign it every meeting**, and the signature is the point: *"it's the cover your \\*\\*\\*, it's the CYA… the insurance company is looking to see who's at fault, and the MSP needs to make sure they're not the negligent party."* Independent corroboration of its centrality: HaloPSA's own recommended agenda gives "Review of Exposure and Risk" the single longest block, 45 of ~110 minutes. This is why the register can't be treated as "a table the AI fills in" — it is the legal-adjacent heart of the ritual, and everything that drafts into it inherits that weight. **The two living documents beside it:** the technology roadmap and the technology budget (the CFO asking for a copy of the budget is the observed buy-in moment). Must 5: register + roadmap at minimum, or it isn't an EBR.

The whole template, classified (Maister's job-shop vs production-line, so we automate the right rows):

| Tabs | Class |
|---|---|
| Agenda · Current Climate · Asset Review · Employee data-pull · **Action Items ("turn every action item into a ticket")** · Next Meeting · KPIs | **Production line — automate** |
| Risk & Security Review · Compliancy Review · Technology Roadmap | **Hybrid — draft the entries, protect the judgment** |
| Business Priorities & Goals · Referrals & Testimonials · Technology Budget | **Brains work — do not template the conversation** |

Automating the left column is what buys back time *for* the right column. That's the mechanism, not a feature.

## 06 · WHAT WE CAN PULL WITH OUR THINGS.

She named the sources herself, and they're ours: **N-central** (assets, warranties, EOL) · **N-sight** · **Adlumin** (health report, HIPAA/compliance assessments, real threat telemetry) · **Cove** (backup success, DR readiness) · **the PSA as the aggregator** (tickets, recurring root-causes). First-party, all of it — chapter 1's unfair-advantage argument, now with a practitioner's shopping list attached. The prep phase is ~8 data pulls across these systems, currently done by hand by a technician who should be on the desk.

What we *can't* pull, per the blueprint: **business priorities and goals** — "there is no system of record" for the client's ambitions. That's a conversation. What we can do instead (co-designed live with Chris in the room): the **industry-insight agent** — web-search the client's industry, "here are the top three trends," job postings, news. Anti-vanilla-marketing. *"You get me. Why would I leave you for anybody talking generic?"*

**Sidebar — what we killed on the way** *(the corrections layer, because chapter 1 promised sourced-or-dead)*: ☠ "EBR-doers are 2.5× more profitable" — misattributed, retired. ☠ "ScalePad $300+/user/mo" — matches no published price; real entry $250/mo per-client blocks. ☠ "Three MSP lawsuits from irregular meetings" — two findable, neither about cadence. ⚠ The 3–7h prep figure — teacher-student echo, not triangulation. Directional only, labeled forever.

## 06b · THE TRANSLATION LAYER — we're the experts on their customers.

The pull is the easy half. The product is the **translation** — and the translation has a clean division of expertise:

**We hold the general. The MSP holds the particular.** N-able's side is everything true *across* clients and verticals: what the telemetry means, rendered in her grammar (data → risk → what it means to you → consequence if unactioned) · industry intelligence ("here are the top three trends in dental — are you seeing this?") · and the thing only we can ever hold, **base-wide comparison** — "here's how your profile compares across our whole base." Chapter 1 called that the SOC pattern corpus. This chapter's read: it's the expertise product. Nobody else sits on cross-MSP, cross-vertical telemetry *plus* a Head Nerd enablement practice — being the expert on MSPs' customers-in-general is a moat nobody self-builds in Cloud in a day.

The MSP's side is the particular: this client, this history, this relationship, this room. The industry trend is what makes the MSP sound like a consultant instead of a vendor — *"You get me. Why would I leave you for anybody talking generic?"* — and the client who feels known refers ("dentists know everybody"). Stefanie's nine hours of marketing training, one takeaway: **know your customer, know the industry, know their business.** The kit's job is making that knowable in fifteen minutes.

## 07 · WHAT THAT PROBABLY LOOKS LIKE.

*(Prototyping happens in its own pass — this is the shape the evidence buys.)*

The draft **lands assembled.** Production-line tabs pre-filled from our stack. The register drafted in her insight grammar — **data → risk → what it means to you → impact and consequences if unactioned** — with red/yellow/green grades ("I get very excited by colors… or give me a grade") and talking points written in business outcomes, never metrics.

Over every machine claim, the **defensibility tier** — Measured / Benchmarked / Estimated / Unverified — already implemented over the ledger's `baseline_source`. Headline off *Measured only*. **Hard gate on Unverified** — a warning that requires no action reads as permission, so it isn't a warning, it's a block. And the same gate on *us*: if a draft surfaces an upsell recommendation, it's visibly flagged as commercial — N-able's interest never launders as neutral risk analysis.

And the draft is **chooser-shaped, not prose-shaped** — the MSP as editor-in-chief, not author (too slow) and not reader (too passive, and legally naked):

- Register rows arrive **ranked, with include/exclude** — the machine proposes the order; the MSP decides what leads. Choosing what to raise is a liability judgment (Job 3), so it must be theirs.
- Talking points come in **two registers** — Jonathan spec'd it in June: "audience-adaptive: CFO gets ROI, owner gets strategy." Same data, pick the story.
- The industry brief offers **five trends; the MSP picks three** — the pick is what makes it theirs in the room.
- Recommendations carry the commercial flag; **accepting one is an explicit choice, never a default.**

The operator reviews, chooses, owns the voice. **Auto-draft, human-clears** — the only pattern on file that survives both the trust evidence and the legal read. The seam between our general expertise and their particular judgment *is* the product surface.

## 08 · WHAT THE OUTPUT IS.

Not a report. **Two artifacts and a residue:**

1. **The internal working document** — candid prep notes, operator judgments, "things that haven't happened yet."
2. **The client-facing review** — business-language, forward-looking, signable register.

Two distinct objects in the data model. **Never one document with a redaction toggle** — a Tier-1 prototype made exactly that mistake, council caught it, and an accidental un-toggle is a client-visible incident. (Still n=1 — this is Vanessa's practice, and Stefanie never distinguishes surfaces. Flagged, not settled.)

And the residue, which is where faith lives or dies: **action items become PSA tickets** (Stefanie's #1 killer when absent — and exactly one product in the entire market actually does writeback today) · **the next meeting is booked before anyone leaves the room** (highest-leverage free step in the cycle) · the signed register goes in the file · the KPI tab ticks (retention, referrals, opportunities, NPS).

## 09 · THE JOURNEY.

The service blueprint holds the whole ritual: **26 service moments, 7 stages** — Entice · Enter · Schedule · **Prep-Assemble (×8 data pulls)** · Prep-Judge · **Live Meeting (×12 moments)** · Follow-up · Extend.

Where the time actually goes: prep-assemble — the eight pulls, the hidden second person. Where the value actually happens: the twelve live-meeting moments, which are human and stay human. Where trust actually leaks: follow-up — mechanizable, and mostly not done.

Build coverage, counted honestly: **0 built · 1 specced · 8 adjacent · 17 absent.** The one specced item (QBR Advisor) had 0 of 40 tasks done at last count. The gap is subject matter, not mechanism.

## 10 · WHERE OUR THING FITS IN.

On the blueprint: we own **prep-assemble** (the eight pulls, drafted register, graded insights), we assist **prep-judge** (talking points, industry insights), we keep our hands off the **live meeting**, and we mechanize **follow-up** (tickets, booking, the signed record).

And the substrate already exists: **Runbook Engine 49/49 built. Value Ledger 52/52 built — currently rendering $0 because no producer is wired.** A QBR runbook is a *wiring job on existing plumbing*, not a build from zero.

The extension she handed us unprompted: **it's not one coworker, it's three** — the EBR · a pre-sales first-meeting aggregator ("an MSP will win or lose the deal based on the quality of that first meeting") · a cyber/AI readiness assessment feeding the proposal *and* the register. Same data spine. Three doors. And note her sequencing pedagogy while we're here: **manual first, software later** — the tool should fit the process, not mash the process into the tool. (Our build shipped substrate before workflow. Her sequencing, inverted. Named, not hidden.)

## 11 · WHAT DOES WINNING AND GOOD LOOK LIKE?

**Good is testable, today, against a static prototype.** The eight Musts each carry a grep-able check: business-outcome-to-metric sentence ratio (Must 1) · artifact freshness (2) · ticket-conversion within N days (3) · cadence vs the client's own tier (4) · both artifacts present (5) · internal-vs-export content diff (6) · weekly load vs the observed ceiling (7) · liability-language flag (8). Plus the three pre-code tests: cognitive walkthrough as Vanessa, 5-second test on the confirm-gate, content audit on a sample draft. **The 15-minute bar, rescoped by July:** fifteen minutes of *review after machine-assisted prep* — not fifteen minutes total. The old framing was incompatible with the workload it claimed to compress.

**Winning is a branch decision that hasn't been made.** Two concepts survived Idea Chess: **Package 3 — N-able Native** (~0.9% naive survival, near-term lead, no normalization dependency, dies at Adopt for mixed stacks) and **Package 5 — Cross-Vendor** (~0.43–0.64% floor, the fleet synthesis's "most winnable," gated entirely on the legal read). The council split on what that gate even *is* — market timing (Disruptive Innovation) vs chokepoint control (Value Chain) — and that's a decision, not a gap.

**And honestly: the board may need re-setting.** The last Idea Chess pass predates everything in this chapter — the interview, the three-coworker family, the prep-data bottleneck, the confidence-vs-time twist. **Concepts probably need another round** with July's evidence on the table.

### THE SHAPE IT MIGHT ALL ADD UP TO *(Beth's leap, 07-31 — on the Idea Chess board now, unscored as of this draft)*

Maybe none of this is a meeting-prep product. **Maybe it's continuous visibility — knowing what's going on with the businesses, easily, all the time — and the QBR is just the quarterly render.** The reframe dissolves the oldest divergence in the corpus: QBR tops what buyers want lifted off them (drag-rank 2.90) while Env Health tops what they rate most valuable per agent (3.59) — under this framing they were never two products, just technical visibility and business visibility of the same layer. It even explains the churn trigger: "I never hear from my MSP" is a visibility deficit running the *other* way — the client can't see the invisible work. The living documents Stefanie ran a hundred accounts on were the manual prototype of exactly this. Chapter 1's "six coworkers feed the capstone" stops being a story overlay and becomes literal architecture. Death squares to respect: "build it into the RMM" caps willingness-to-pay for visibility features, and an always-on data layer makes the legal surface *bigger*, not smaller. The re-run will say what survives.

**What the ugly thing lets us ask** — the questions become instruments instead of gates:

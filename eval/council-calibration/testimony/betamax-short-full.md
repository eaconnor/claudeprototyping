# Council Testimony — Betamax brief (`betamax-short.brief.md`)
**Gate 1 status: unverified.** The brief is a one-line product/format description ("first mass-market home VCR format, 1-hour max recording, closed/slow licensing to other manufacturers") — not a sourced customer problem statement. No named customer, no research citation, no data on what buyers actually wanted. Council voices below are testifying against a spec, not against sourced pain. Treat as historical case analysis, not validated brief.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — Betamax solved the wrong version of the recording job

- The functional job most consumers actually hired a home VCR for was "let me capture a full-length broadcast (movie, football game, sitcom block) without babysitting the machine." A 1-hour cap fires the product mid-job for any content longer than an hour — which is most of what people wanted to record. [CS: HIGH — Betamax's original max was ~1 hour vs. VHS's 2+ hour cassettes, widely documented]
- Sony optimized for a different job than the one being hired for: picture/tape quality and format control, an engineering-pride job, not the consumer's actual "don't miss the second half of the game" job.
- The existing "solution" being fired wasn't a competing product — it was appointment viewing (be in front of the TV when it airs). Betamax under-delivered against that displaced behavior for anything over an hour, so some households simply didn't switch behavior at all.
- Brief gives zero language for what job Sony *believed* it was serving vs. what job the market wanted. That gap is inferred from history, not stated in the brief.

**Strongest objection:** JTBD needs a named customer segment and their hiring criteria to be rigorous. This brief has neither — everything above is pattern-matched from known VCR-format history, not sourced from this spec.

---

### Service Blueprint — the failure lives backstage, not on the box

- Frontstage: a consumer compares recording time and price at retail. Backstage: which manufacturers get licensed to build decks, and how fast — and that's exactly the lever the brief names as "closed/slow licensing."
- Closed/slow licensing throttled the backstage ecosystem (OEM partners, blank-tape suppliers, video rental stock) invisibly to the person standing in the store. The consumer experienced this months later as "the rental store doesn't carry Betamax tapes of the movie I want" — a backstage decision surfacing as a frontstage disappointment with no visible handoff explaining why.
- The brief is silent on content licensing (movie studios), rental distribution, and manufacturing partnerships — the support processes that determined the actual outcome of this format more than the 1-hour spec did.
- No mention of what happens when a Betamax household needs to trade tapes with a VHS household — a backstage incompatibility with real frontstage friction (can't share, can't rent compatible content).

**Strongest objection:** this framework assumes a service backstage worth mapping. The brief gives one sentence and no ecosystem detail — the "backstage" here is entirely reconstructed from outside knowledge, not sourced from the spec.

---

### Self-Determination Theory — a forced fit, but the friction is real where it lands

- Autonomy: a 1-hour ceiling means the user can't set-and-forget a recording longer than that — they're forced to physically intervene mid-task (swap tape, restart), which is the opposite of "I'm in control of this."
- Competence: a tape running out mid-game or mid-movie reads to the user as personal failure ("I should have checked the length") even though it's a hardware constraint — the device engineers a false sense of user incompetence.
- Relatedness: closed licensing shrank the pool of people who owned compatible decks and could swap tapes/recommendations — weakening the social, shareable dimension of the format relative to a more open standard.
- None of this is stated anywhere in the brief; it's inferred from the two named constraints (1-hour limit, closed licensing).

**Strongest objection:** SDT is built for intrinsic motivation in tasks and interfaces, not consumer hardware-format economics. Applying autonomy/competence/relatedness to a licensing strategy is a stretch — flag this fit as weak rather than pretend it's a clean read.

---

### Disruptive Innovation — ambiguous who's playing which role

- Sony's move reads as a sustaining strategy: better engineering, tighter control, premium quality, aimed at the segment that already valued image fidelity — not a disruptive play into a new/underserved market.
- JVC's VHS, with looser/faster licensing, recruited more OEMs (Panasonic, RCA, and others) faster — that's the disruptive lever: a simpler, cheaper, more available product that attacked market share from a broader base, not superior tech.
- Who loses market position if the brief's described strategy holds: Sony does, specifically because "closed/slow licensing" caps the ecosystem size just as a rival opens theirs — this is the mechanism, named directly in the brief's own second clause.
- The brief doesn't name an incumbent to defend — there's no "existing market" being disrupted here; this is a two-sided format war, not a classic incumbent-vs-entrant story, and forcing that binary onto it is imprecise.

**Strongest objection:** the framework requires an incumbent with something to defend. This brief describes a market-entry format war between two new entrants — Disruptive Innovation's core lens (protect vs. attack an installed base) doesn't cleanly map here.

---

### Value Chain — closed licensing protects the wrong margin

- The capability described inserts at the home-entertainment node between broadcast/studio distribution and the consumer — the "time-shifting" link in the chain.
- Closed/slow licensing protects Sony's margin per unit (control the component, capture royalty) at the cost of ecosystem scale — fewer OEMs means smaller total volume, higher per-unit cost, and less negotiating leverage with studios and rental chains.
- The chokepoint Sony held was the technology patent; the chokepoint JVC's approach ceded control of but captured instead was distribution — more manufacturers, more shelf space, more rental stock. Distribution chokepoint proved more decisive than the technology chokepoint.
- Margin effect is inferred, not sourced: the brief gives no royalty rates, unit costs, or licensing terms — "closed/slow" is a directional signal, not a number.

**Strongest objection:** every margin claim above is inference from known format-war history, not data in this brief. There is no pricing, royalty, or cost line here to verify against.

---

> **JTBD and Service Blueprint diverge on where the failure actually lives.** JTBD locates it in the functional job unmet (1-hour recording ceiling — a product-spec problem). Service Blueprint locates it in the backstage ecosystem (licensing, distribution, rental stock — an operations/partnership problem). This is a decision about what the brief owner believes killed Betamax, not a gap either framework can resolve on its own.

> **Disruptive Innovation and Value Chain diverge on what "control" bought Sony.** Disruptive Innovation frames closed licensing as a sustaining-strategy misread of the market (optimized for the wrong customer). Value Chain frames the same decision as a chokepoint trade (traded distribution scale for royalty control) — a rational margin play that happened to lose. Same fact, opposite verdict on whether it was a strategic error or a strategic bet that didn't pay off. Route this to whoever owns the "what does closed licensing mean for us" call in the actual brief this Betamax case is standing in for.

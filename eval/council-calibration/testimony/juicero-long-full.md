### Jobs-to-Be-Done — the brief conflates "get juice from a pack" with "operate a $400 machine"

The functional job is plausibly "produce a glass of fresh juice with less mess and time than a traditional juicer." The emotional/social job is more likely doing the real work here — signaling health-consciousness and premium lifestyle investment, adjacent to the juice-bar habit it's meant to displace. The brief names the fired solution implicitly (Gate 1: "traditional juicer," "juice bars") but never asks the sharper JTBD question: is the job "extract juice from produce" or "extract juice from *this proprietary pack*"? If packs are hand-squeezable (Gate 2's own `[?]`), the actual job — squeezing a bag — doesn't require the hired product at all, meaning the $400 machine may be solving a job that doesn't exist independent of the packaging Press itself created.

**Strongest objection:** JTBD would refuse to score this brief on convenience-value until the hand-squeeze question is resolved, because right now the brief can't say whether the customer is hiring the machine or hiring the pack. Those have wildly different willingness-to-pay curves, and the brief's OKR key result (`[?]`) bets on the more expensive one without evidence.

### Service Blueprint — the one failure path the brief itself names has no backstage response

Frontstage is thin and clean: scan pack, press, drink. Backstage carries real weight the brief doesn't blueprint at all — proprietary pack manufacturing, a QR authentication layer that must distinguish "genuine" packs from anything else, and a wifi dependency for a kitchen appliance. None of the standard support processes are addressed: connectivity failure, QR misread, subscription pause/cancel, hardware warranty/recall.

The most glaring gap: Gate 3 and Failure Mode #2 both flag that if packs can be hand-squeezed, "the subscription lock-in reads as exploitative rather than convenient" — that is a customer-discovery event with real support and PR consequences, and the brief has zero backstage plan for it. A blueprint pass would treat that discovery moment as the single highest-risk touchpoint in the whole service, not a footnote.

**Strongest objection:** the brief documents the failure mode but doesn't blueprint the response to it. There is no defined frontstage/backstage behavior for the day a customer squeezes the pack by hand on camera. That's not a missing detail — it's the whole risk.

### Self-Determination Theory — the counter-metric is an admission the design withholds competence, not builds it

Autonomy is constrained by design: proprietary packs, no generic-produce compatibility, wifi requirement, QR gating — the user cannot substitute inputs or operate offline. Competence is nearly disabled by design too — "insert pack, press button" has almost no skill ceiling, which is a different failure than *low* competence-support: it's competence made irrelevant, arguably lower relatedness-to-craft than home juicing itself offered. Relatedness is thin — the ongoing relationship is a subscription billing cycle, not a connection to any broader practice or community.

The counter-metric is the tell: "perceived value of the machine must not collapse if the packs can be squeezed by hand without it." Read through SDT, that's not a convenience feature — it's a design goal to make the customer feel *incapable* without the machine, i.e., competence is being suppressed as a business strategy, not supported as a UX goal.

**Strongest objection:** no design mitigation is named anywhere in the brief for the fact that its own success metric depends on customers *not* discovering they're capable of doing the task by hand. That's not an SDT gap — it's an SDT trap, named by the brief's own counter-metric and left unaddressed.

### Disruptive Innovation — this is a sustaining premium bet wearing disruption language, and the brief doesn't know which one it is

A $400 hardware entry price with recurring proprietary-consumable lock-in is not a low-end disruptive pattern (cheaper, simpler, new-market-creating) — it's priced and structured like a sustaining premium play (razor-and-blades) aimed at existing juice-bar/blender customers. Who loses market position depends entirely on the unresolved hand-squeeze question: if the machine adds real functional value over hand pressure, juice bars lose some at-home-occasions share; if it doesn't, *nobody* loses anything, because Press has added no capability the market didn't already have cheaper (a blender) or better (a bar).

What the incumbent has to defend also isn't named: juice bars defend "someone else does the mess," blenders defend price-and-familiarity. The brief never states which of these Press is actually attacking.

**Strongest objection:** the brief's OKR framing ("bring cold-press-quality juice into the home") implies disruption of juice bars, but the pricing and lock-in structure is a sustaining-incumbent play against blenders. Those are different competitive bets requiring different evidence, and the brief has picked neither explicitly.

> **Named divergence — Disruptive Innovation vs. Jobs-to-Be-Done:** Disruptive Innovation reads the $400 price point as evidence this is a sustaining play against juice bars (premium, not low-end-disruptive). JTBD reads the same price point as evidence the *emotional/social* job (premium wellness signaling) may justify willingness-to-pay independent of functional juice-bar displacement at all. One frame says the price is wrong for the stated competitive target; the other says the competitive target may be the wrong lens entirely. This is a decision about which market Press is actually competing in, not a gap — route it to the brief owner.

### Value Chain — the QR authentication layer is the chokepoint, and its existence as a chokepoint is the one thing the brief admits is unverified

Press inserts itself vertically between produce supply and home consumption, capturing margin at the packing layer (proprietary, single-use, no generic compatibility per Gate 3) rather than at the hardware layer alone. This is textbook razor-and-blades margin protection: hardware near cost or subsidized, recurring consumable carries the margin. The chokepoint is the QR-authentication step — whoever controls whether a pack is "genuine" controls the recurring revenue, not the customer holding the produce.

But Gate 2, Gate 3, and Failure Mode #2 all converge on the same unresolved fact: whether that chokepoint is *real* (requires machine pressing force) or *manufactured* (packs are squeezable by hand, meaning the QR gate is enforcing scarcity that doesn't functionally exist). A value-chain analysis of a chokepoint whose physical necessity is unverified is analysis of a hypothesis, not a business model.

**Strongest objection:** every margin claim in this reading collapses to the same unresolved `[?]` the other four voices also hit — Value Chain can describe the *intended* chokepoint mechanics precisely, but cannot confirm the chokepoint exists outside of QR-code enforcement, which is a policy choice, not a physical constraint.

> **Named divergence — Value Chain vs. Service Blueprint:** Value Chain reads the QR/proprietary-pack system as rational margin-protection strategy — a chokepoint deliberately engineered to capture recurring revenue. Service Blueprint reads the identical mechanism as an invisible backstage control imposed on the customer with no visible justification and no failure-path support. Same mechanism, opposite verdict: smart chokepoint economics vs. undisclosed customer-trust liability. This is a strategy-vs-trust tradeoff, not a factual disagreement — route it to the brief owner rather than resolving it here.

# Council Testimony — Roadster / Tesla Long Brief

**Brief:** `tesla-long.brief.md` — Roadster as low-volume EV to fund mass-market EV
**Gate 1 status:** Not empty, but mixed. The problem statement rests on one [D] claim (EV1 discontinuation, publicly documented) and one [A] claim ("no car has proven EVs can be aspirational rather than a compromise"). The OKR key result, counter metric, and both failure-mode items are [A]/[?]. Roughly half the tagged claims in this brief are assumed or unknown, not sourced. Council voices below are commentary against a partially-sourced problem — treat accordingly, especially anything downstream of the [A] sequencing thesis.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — Roadster is hired to do a job the mass-market buyer doesn't have

The brief conflates two different customers' jobs without naming the split. The Roadster's actual buyer hires it for status/performance signaling ("I bought the fastest car, and it happens to be electric") — an emotional and social job. The brief's OKR frames this as proving a functional job ("EVs can be desirable") on behalf of a buyer who isn't in the room yet: the future mass-market customer. [A]

The brief never states what gets fired. If the Roadster buyer's prior solution was a Porsche or a first sports car, that's a status-job swap, not an EV-adoption swap — the golf-cart image problem named in Gate 1 doesn't get solved by this customer's purchase at all, since this customer was never carrying that image. [A]

"Repurposed laptop-style battery cells rather than purpose-built automotive cells" [A] is a Gate 2 build choice justified nowhere against the actual job. If the job is "prove desirability," cell sourcing is invisible to the buyer and doesn't serve that job — it serves a cost/speed-to-market job that the brief doesn't name as a job at all.

**Strongest objection:** The brief's counter_metric and OKR key result assume the low-volume buyer's satisfaction transfers as proof toward the mass-market buyer. JTBD would say these are different jobs held by different customers, and desirability proven in one does not transfer to price-sensitivity solved in the other. The brief treats "prove desirability" as a single continuous funnel; JTBD sees two disconnected hiring decisions.

---

### Service Blueprint — the backstage the brief names is the frontstage risk it doesn't

"No dealer network — sells direct" [A] is stated as a Gate 3 fact with no service-layer consequence attached. Direct sales collapses the traditional frontstage/backstage split (dealer handles financing, service, warranty claims, trade-in) onto a company with zero prior automotive service infrastructure. The brief's "What's missing" section names regulatory/dealer-franchise-law risk [?] but stops at the legal question — it doesn't ask who does service, recalls, or warranty work when there is no dealer backstage to absorb it.

"Sourcing chassis/components from a low-volume specialist (Lotus)" [D] is a backstage handoff to a third party with its own service and supply constraints, and the brief doesn't surface whether that handoff is visible or invisible to the customer — a Roadster owner likely doesn't know how much of the car's fulfillment chain sits outside company control.

Nothing in Gate 3 addresses what happens when a customer's car breaks down. For a company betting its funding runway on this model selling well and generating trust for the mass-market follow-on, unresolved service backstage is a brand risk the brief doesn't score.

**Strongest objection:** The brief scores Gate 3 on manufacturing/sourcing risk only. It is silent on service, warranty, and support — the parts of a service blueprint most likely to determine whether early buyers become advocates or detractors ahead of the mass-market launch this whole sequencing bet depends on.

---

### Self-Determination Theory — the buyer's autonomy is served; the mission's competence claim is not yet earned

For the actual Roadster buyer, autonomy and competence are well served: direct sales (no dealer haggling), a car chosen for status and performance where the buyer feels in control of a distinctive purchase. Relatedness is arguably the whole point of the buy — signaling identity within an environmentalist-but-high-performance in-group.

At the organizational level, though, the brief's own OKR ("prove EVs can be desirable... before attempting mass-market") [A] is a competence claim the company hasn't earned yet — it's borrowing competence from a hypothesis, not from evidence. That's a mismatch between what SDT would ask of a *company's* trust model (does the org have real competence evidence) and what the brief supplies (an assumed key result).

The counter_metric ("capital burn must not outpace ability to reach a second, cheaper model") [?] is explicitly unknown. SDT would flag this as an autonomy risk not for the customer but for the org itself: a company operating without a verified competence signal on its own survival metric is not "in control" of its own trajectory — it's making an autonomy claim on capital markets it hasn't tested.

**Strongest objection:** SDT applies naturally to the end user here, and the brief passes on that axis. Where it fails is applying the same rigor to the organization's own agency — the brief asserts competence ("prove desirability") without an evidence base, and the counter-metric that would validate organizational competence is marked [?], unresolved.

### Disruptive Innovation — this is neither sustaining nor low-end disruptive; it's new-market entry dressed as sequencing strategy

Christensen's framework doesn't map cleanly here, and that's worth naming directly. The Roadster is not sustaining innovation (it doesn't serve existing EV customers better — EV1 customers were leased and discontinued, there's no installed base) [D]. It's also not classic low-end disruption (a simpler, cheaper product attacking incumbents from below) — the Roadster is more expensive and more exclusive than incumbent gas sports cars, not less. [A]

What the brief is actually describing is high-end market entry funding a *future* low-end/new-market disruption. That's a legitimate strategy but not the one Christensen's model predicts succeeds — his research on disruption specifically shows entrants win by being worse-but-cheaper-and-improving, not better-but-pricier funding a future cheaper version. The brief's own "failure modes" section (capital intensity outpacing the pivot) is exactly the risk profile of a strategy that departs from the disruption playbook it's implicitly invoking via the mission language ("accelerate transition"). [A]

Incumbents (GM, and by extension every legacy automaker) have nothing to defend yet at this stage — a $100k low-volume sports car doesn't threaten their volume business. The brief doesn't identify who loses market position if Roadster succeeds, because at this gate, nobody does. The real disruptive threat (to incumbent automakers) is deferred entirely to the unproven, unbuilt second model.

**Strongest objection:** The brief implicitly borrows disruption-theory credibility ("accelerate the world's transition") for a move that is high-end market entry, not disruption from below. Christensen's own case evidence (which this brief's Gate 3 gestures at via DeLorean/Fisker) is that well-funded, non-disruptive premium entrants in capital-intensive incumbent industries fail at high rates — the brief cites the failure pattern but doesn't reconcile it with the strategy it's defending.

---

### Value Chain — Roadster buys a funding position, not a margin position

The Roadster inserts at the R&D-funding stage of the value chain, not at a customer-value-delivery stage that compounds. Its purpose per the brief is capital generation to fund a later product [A] — margin effect on the Roadster line itself is close to irrelevant to the mission; what matters is whether Roadster revenue net of manufacturing cost funds enough runway to reach model two. The brief never states the actual margin math, tagging it [?] via the capital-burn counter-metric.

The chokepoint in this value chain is not held by the company — it's held by Lotus (chassis/component supply) [D] and by whoever controls automotive-grade battery cell cost curves going forward, since the brief admits current cells are repurposed laptop cells, not a proprietary automotive supply chain [A]. The brief's own failure mode #2 (battery cost curve) [?] is a tacit admission that the company does not yet control its own critical input.

No dealer network removes a traditional value-chain intermediary and its margin take, which should show up as either higher margin per unit or lower price to the customer — the brief doesn't state which, or whether it matters at this gate given the company doesn't yet control the cell-cost chokepoint anyway.

**Strongest objection:** The brief frames Roadster's economics only in relation to itself (does it fund a future model) and never states where actual margin lives in this chain or who currently controls the chokepoint input (cells, chassis). Without that, "capital burn must not outpace the pivot" [?] is a runway statement, not a value-chain statement — the brief is missing the analysis of whether the company can ever *own* enough of this chain to reach the margin structure mass-market EVs require.

---

> **Disruptive Innovation** and **Value Chain** converge on the same underlying gap from different angles — both land on "the company doesn't control its critical inputs (cells, chassis) yet," but Disruptive Innovation frames this as a strategic-category mismatch (this isn't disruption, it's premium entry) while Value Chain frames it as a chokepoint-ownership problem (funding doesn't equal control). These are different diagnoses of the same missing evidence — not a contradiction to resolve, a gap to route to the brief owner.

> **Jobs-to-Be-Done** and **Self-Determination Theory** diverge on the buyer: JTBD says the Roadster buyer's job is disconnected from the mass-market buyer's job (transfer doesn't hold), while SDT says the Roadster buyer's autonomy/competence/relatedness needs are well served *on their own terms*, independent of whether that experience transfers to a future customer. This is a decision about what "success" means at this gate — is Roadster judged as a standalone product experience (SDT: it passes) or as a proof-of-transfer instrument (JTBD: transfer is unproven) — not a gap. Route to the brief owner.

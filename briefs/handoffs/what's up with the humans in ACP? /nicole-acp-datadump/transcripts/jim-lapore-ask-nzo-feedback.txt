What Jim Liked About N‑zo
Strong value for lifecycle planning
Jim repeatedly emphasized that N‑zo was especially helpful for lifecycle planning and asset evaluation.

“I did use it several times in a kind of a light, we call it life cycle planning here.”
“For our life cycle planning process that Enzo would be valuable to analyze.”
“I can see us using that as part of our planning process.”

High‑quality recommendations and breakdowns
He was impressed with how clearly N‑zo explained why machines should be replaced.

“It did a really good job at that.”
“It gave us a nice breakdown of all the machines… I would recommend these because of age… four gigs of RAM… and broke it down.”

Surfaced insights he would have missed manually
N‑zo caught issues that did not stand out in his own review.

“It was able to tell me, hey, you know what? This laptop only has a Celeron processor… You might want to replace that and that didn’t bubble up on my initial review.”

Clear time savings
Jim was very concrete about efficiency gains.

“It saved definitely saved us time.”
“I would say 20 minutes probably down to.”
“It came back with the information… in less than a minute.”

Scales across customers
He didn’t just test once. He reused it across multiple environments.

“I did it several times for several different customers over the course of the trial.”

Speed met expectations
Response time was solid and comparable to other AI tools he uses.

“No, I was happy with the speed.”
“I didn’t have any qualms about the response time.”

Accuracy and reliability
He explicitly called out confidence in the results.

“I had no issues with speed or accuracy.”

Useful for customer-facing planning conversations
N‑zo supported the work he does to build and present technology plans to customers.

“We’re evaluating their infrastructure and creating a technology plan for the upcoming year.”
“Part of that is evaluating their computer infrastructure and making recommendations.”

Willingness to adopt when generally available
This wasn’t just a nice experiment. He sees it as something they would actually use.

“Yeah, I can see us using that as part of our planning process.”

Exportable outputs are acceptable
He confirmed that simple export formats would work for their workflow.

“Exportable into a Word format is fine.”

 
WHAT DID JIM NOT LIKE

 

Everything Jim Did Not Like or Found Limiting
Missing access to software asset data
This was his strongest and most repeated concern.

“It didn’t have access to the software assets, but it did to the hardware.”
“That would be nice.”
“The data’s there, it must be somewhere different that it’s harder for it to grab.”

He specifically wanted:

Installed software visibility
Versions
Cross‑customer querying
“It would be nice… to easily see across everybody who has this piece of software installed.”
“Who has maybe… Adobe 2017 that’s gonna be going off support.”

He emphasized this multiple times:

“I really must have hit me with the software… I mentioned it three times in my notes here.”

Inability to answer firmware‑related questions
He tested this intentionally and noted the limitation.

“I tried seeing if it could determine which computers needed a firmware update.”
“It was not able to do that.”

He clarified that this was not unexpected, but still a gap.

Ambiguous categorization in memory breakdowns
He found the way RAM ranges were grouped confusing and imprecise.

“It broke down the computers into what I would consider ambiguous category.”
“Here are the computers that have between 4 and 8 gigs of RAM.”
“If they have eight, are they in the 4 to 8 or are they in the 8 to 16?”
“That gave me some ambiguous data there.”

No way to start a new chat / clear context
This was framed as a UX issue and compared directly to Copilot.

“When I’m using Copilot, there’s a button to start a new chat.”
“There’s no button like that that I could find in Enzo.”
“That would be a nice addition as well.”

Poor discoverability / placement of N‑zo in the UI
His team struggled to even find it.

“Every time I was, ‘Where is it? I can’t find it.’”
“It’s like it’s on the asset tab, guys.”

He tied this directly to reduced usage:

“If it was more prominent, it would get more use, I’m sure.”

Team onboarding clarity (indirect issue)
While Jim personally was fine, his team struggled.

“I didn’t have any problem.”
“They had a little bit more difficulty with it.”
“It may have been that I didn’t communicate that great to my team.”

This is still a signal of friction at scale.

Lack of software‑side lifecycle planning
He framed current value as hardware‑only and future‑incomplete.

“The hardware side, at least for now, and eventually the software side.”

 
Data We Should Add (In Order)
1. Software asset inventory
This was the most explicit and repeated gap.

“It didn’t have access to the software assets.”
“I mentioned the software… I mentioned it three times in my notes here.”

Specifically:

Installed software per machine
Software visibility equivalent to hardware assets
2. Software version data
Not just what is installed, but which version.

“You want to know the software that’s installed on a machine and the version.”

This is foundational for lifecycle planning and risk conversations.

3. Cross‑customer software querying
He wants to ask questions across all clients, not one at a time.

“Easily see across everybody who has this piece of software installed.”
“Who has maybe… Adobe 2017 that’s gonna be going off support.”

This is a scale requirement, not a convenience feature.

4. Firmware status / firmware update data
He tested this intentionally and noted the limitation.

“I tried seeing if it could determine which computers needed a firmware update.”
“It was not able to do that.”

Even though he said he didn’t expect it, it is still a clearly stated data gap.

5. More precise memory (RAM) categorization
The issue here is data clarity, not absence.

“It broke down the computers into what I would consider ambiguous category.”
“If they have eight, are they in the 4 to 8 or are they in the 8 to 16?”

This implies the need for:

Exact values
Non‑overlapping ranges
Clear thresholds
6. Expanded lifecycle data beyond hardware
He framed current value as incomplete without software.

“The hardware side, at least for now, and eventually the software side.”

This reinforces that lifecycle planning data must include:

Hardware + software together
End‑of‑support signals tied to both
7. Vulnerability data (forward‑looking confirmation)
This came up at the end as something he was told was coming and responded positively to.

“We are including vulnerability information over the next sprint.”

While not a complaint, it is implicitly validated as expected and relevant data for his use case.

“When I’m using like Copilot, there’s a button to, you know, start a new chat so it forgets the history of your previous chat.”

5. Team‑level onboarding friction (UX clarity)

# Council Testimony — Webvan Long Brief

### 1. Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen / Ulwick) — The job is asserted, not interviewed

- The brief hires "automated warehouses + 30-minute delivery windows" against a job statement that is itself [A] — "grocery shopping is a low-value chore consumers want removed." No evidence this is the actual hierarchy of jobs (functional: replenishment; emotional: control over freshness/quality; social: none named) versus an assumed one borrowed from dot-com enthusiasm.
- The fired incumbent isn't named. Is Webvan firing the weekly car trip to the supermarket, or firing a *habit* (browsing, touch/smell selection of produce) that resists automation? The brief treats grocery shopping as a monolithic chore, but produce/perishables carry a different job (trust, quality control) than staples (functional, low-emotion). No segmentation.
- 30-minute delivery windows solve a job nobody stated needing solved this precisely — the brief never establishes that speed, rather than reliability or price, is the dimension consumers are underserved on. [A]

**Strongest objection:** The whole capital structure — automated warehouses, city-by-city buildout — is sized to a job definition that was never validated against the actual target market. If the job is "save 45 minutes," a $1B automated fulfillment network is wildly over-engineered for the value delivered. JTBD would demand the interview data before the capex commitment, not after.

### 2. Service Blueprint (Shostack / Bitner) — The backstage is the entire bet, and it's invisible to the customer

- Frontstage is thin: order online, 30-minute window, delivery arrives. Backstage is the entire brief — automated warehouse construction, fleet routing, inventory-perishability logistics — none of which the customer sees or is asked to trust. The customer experiences a promise (30 minutes) built on infrastructure that has never operated at scale in even one market. [D]
- No support process is addressed for what happens when the automated warehouse model fails at the line level — substitutions, damaged perishables, missed windows. Grocery has a failure-recovery surface (bruised produce, wrong item, spoiled dairy) that traditional stores handle via a human at customer service; the brief is silent on this backstage.
- The handoff between "automated pick" and "human last-mile delivery" is a seam. Automation errors compound with delivery-window promises — a jammed warehouse pick doesn't just delay backstage, it breaks a frontstage promise (30 minutes) that's core to the value proposition. That fragility isn't named as a risk.

**Strongest objection:** The brief plans city-by-city expansion before the backstage (warehouse automation + perishable logistics) has been proven reliable in one city. Service Blueprint would say: you cannot scale a frontstage promise built on an unproven backstage process. This is Gate 3's failure mode restated in blueprint terms — the brief already half-knows this ("expansion planned ahead of confirming unit economics") but doesn't connect it to operational reliability risk, only capital risk.

### 3. Self-Determination Theory (Deci / Ryan) — Convenience at the cost of competence and autonomy is untested

- Autonomy: ordering groceries online removes the browsing/selection act. For some consumers this is relief (autonomy gained — no forced errand); for others, selection *is* the autonomy (choosing today's produce, comparing prices in-aisle). The brief treats removal of choice as universally welcome. [A]
- Competence: grocery shopping is a domain where many consumers feel competent (they know their store, their routine). Automating it via warehouse-picked substitutions risks eroding that felt competence — the "wrong item" or "no availability" failure mode (Failure Mode 1, cost tolerance) directly undermines competence if substitutions are poor.
- Relatedness: no social/community dimension is addressed at all. Grocery shopping for many is embedded in errands, incidental social contact, neighborhood routine. The brief's mission (remove the chore) is silent on what's lost if that relatedness — however minor — disappears with the errand.

**Strongest objection:** SDT would flag that the brief's entire value proposition rests on an assumption that convenience trumps autonomy/competence trade-offs for the *majority* of a market, with zero segmentation data to support it. This is the same gap JTBD names from a different angle — worth flagging as convergence, not just SDT's own concern.

### 4. Disruptive Innovation (Christensen) — This is sustaining capital deployed as if it were disruptive, and the incumbents aren't threatened yet

- The model is not disruptive in Christensen's sense — it is a *higher-cost, higher-service* substitute for existing grocery retail (automated warehouses, dedicated fleets, tight delivery windows), not a simpler/cheaper offering attacking from below. Disruption theory would predict this lands as a sustaining bet aimed at existing grocery customers who already shop and can already afford a premium — not a new-market or low-end disruption.
- Given the [D] fact that home internet/PC penetration is dial-up-era, the addressable population for online ordering is itself small and skews toward higher-income, already-well-served customers — the opposite of a disruptive footprint (which typically starts by serving overlooked or non-consuming segments).
- Incumbent grocers have low incentive to react: Webvan's cost structure ($1B in warehouses before profitability) is not a cost advantage they need to defend against. Christensen's model would predict incumbents largely ignore this until/unless it proves unit economics — which the brief admits [?] hasn't happened in even one market.

**Strongest objection:** The brief's own OKR ("capture grocery... as an online-first business") reads as land-grab logic borrowed from disruption rhetoric, but the underlying mechanics (high capital, high service cost, premium positioning, dial-up-era addressable market) are sustaining-innovation mechanics dressed in disruptive language. Disruptive Innovation would say: this brief is using the vocabulary of disruption to justify capital intensity that disruption theory would actually flag as a warning sign, not a strength.

> **Named divergence:** Disruptive Innovation and Value Chain diverge on where the risk sits. Disruptive Innovation says the strategic risk is market-fit (wrong customer, wrong cost structure for the addressable population). Value Chain says the strategic risk is chokepoint control (who owns fulfillment infrastructure). These are different bets on what kills the company first — this is a decision for the brief owner, not a gap to resolve here.

### 5. Value Chain (Porter) — Owning the chokepoint before proving the chokepoint is worth owning

- The brief's Gate 2 bet — own the full stack (warehouses, fleet, windows) rather than partner with existing grocers — is a vertical-integration play for chokepoint control over last-mile fulfillment. Porter's margin logic: vertical integration only pays if the owned link captures margin that would otherwise leak to a partner. The brief never establishes that fulfillment is the scarce, high-margin link in this value chain — it might be sourcing/procurement or brand trust instead. [A]
- Grocery is explicitly named as low-margin (Gate 1). Porter would ask: if the category has thin margins industry-wide, does inserting a *more* capital-intensive owned layer (automated warehouses) protect margin or destroy it? The brief's own $1B capital commitment against a low-margin category is a margin-negative signal unaddressed in the brief.
- No control-point analysis of suppliers (grocery wholesalers, perishable distributors) — Webvan owns warehouses but does it own or control upstream sourcing advantage? Without that, the chokepoint it owns (fulfillment) may not be defensible; competitors could replicate delivery logistics without replicating a sourcing edge Webvan doesn't have either.

**Strongest objection:** Value Chain would say the brief conflates "owning infrastructure" with "controlling a chokepoint that generates margin." Building your own automated warehouses is expensive infrastructure ownership, not necessarily value-chain leverage — and in a category already flagged as low-margin, this bet plausibly destroys margin rather than protecting or creating it. That's the inverse of what Gate 2's stated rationale claims.

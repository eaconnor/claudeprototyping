### Jobs-to-Be-Done — the brief states a solution, not a job

- Functional job implied: stop carrying three devices (phone, music player, internet device) and get one. The brief names the *convergence*, never the job it serves — no quote, no "I hate switching devices" moment cited.
- Emotional/social job is entirely absent from the brief. A device this category-defining almost certainly carries a status/identity job (early adopter, taste signal) — but that's inference from outside the brief, not something the text supports. [A]
- What gets fired: keyboard-first devices (physical QWERTY) and stylus-first devices, by the brief's own framing — it defines itself by what it removes. That's a clean fired-solution signal, one of the only concrete JTBD moves the brief actually gives you.

**Strongest objection:** The brief assumes convergence is the job. Nobody asked "please combine my three devices" on the record here — that's Jobs's read of the job, stated as a spec, not evidenced. JTBD run against an assumed job is commentary wearing analysis's clothes.

### Service Blueprint — the brief has no service layer at all

- Zero frontstage/backstage content. The brief describes a device, full stop — no purchase flow, no carrier relationship, no activation, no support model.
- Removing the keyboard and stylus removes the two most tactile, most learnable input methods on the market at the time [CS: HIGH — not sourced by brief]. That's an onboarding cliff the brief never addresses: who teaches someone to type on glass, and what's the fallback when they fail?
- No mention of what happens when the all-touchscreen promise breaks — wet hands, gloves, cracked glass, mistyped autocorrect. A device that eliminates the two most failure-tolerant input modes (a stylus point is precise; a physical key has tactile confirmation) needs a backstage support answer this brief doesn't carry.

**Strongest objection:** This isn't a thin service blueprint — it's an absent one. There's no handoff to name because there's no service described. That's not a nitpick; a device-only brief for a device this behaviorally disruptive is missing exactly the layer that determines whether people succeed with it on day one.

### Self-Determination Theory — competence is the whole bet, and the brief doesn't hedge it

- Competence risk: removing physical keys removes tactile confirmation of input. Until muscle memory forms on glass, felt competence likely *drops* relative to a physical keyboard — the brief treats this as pure gain, no acknowledgment of the learning cost.
- Autonomy: collapsing three devices into one removes the ability to customize per-device (swap a keyboard case, choose a separate music player) — a loss of autonomy for power users, framed by the brief as unambiguous simplification.
- Relatedness: "phone + music player + internet" touches communication and social context directly, but the brief names none of it — no mention of messaging, sharing, or any socially-oriented capability. Relatedness is the least-evidenced of the three needs here.
- No AI trust model applies — this is a pre-AI-era device brief; that plank of the framework has nothing to grip.

**Strongest objection:** SDT needs interaction evidence — does typing on glass actually *feel* competent to a first-time user? — and the brief supplies none. This testimony is inferred from category history, not drawn from anything in the text.

### Disruptive Innovation — the pattern doesn't cleanly fit, and the brief can't settle it

- Removing the keyboard attacks the core differentiator of keyboard-first incumbents directly (BlackBerry/RIM) [CS: HIGH — not sourced by brief]; removing the stylus attacks stylus-first incumbents directly (Palm) [CS: HIGH — not sourced by brief]. That's a frontal attack on incumbent identity, not a low-end sneak-in.
- Classic Christensen disruption enters from below on price or serves a non-consumer with a "good enough" simpler product. The brief gives zero pricing or channel data, so there's no way to confirm this is disruptive-from-below versus a sustaining innovation aimed at the same premium buyer.
- One incumbent this brief attacks is the maker's *own* prior product — a standalone music player gets folded into this device. That's self-cannibalization, a different pattern than "who loses market position" implies.

**Strongest objection:** Without price or channel, the Christensen test can't actually run. Naming BlackBerry and Palm as losers is a plausible market read, not something the brief supports — it's outside-the-brief knowledge doing the analytical work the brief should be doing.

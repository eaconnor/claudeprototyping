**Brief:** New Coke — reformulated recipe replacing original (`eval/council-calibration/briefs/newcoke-long.brief.md`), Gate 30 (early/concept stage).
**Tier: MEAL.**

**Gate 1 status:** Partially sourced, not empty — extensive blind taste-test data exists [D], but it is narrow (single-sip only) and the counter-metric (loyalty/emotional damage) is explicitly [?] unmeasured, with no cited research on brand-identity/nostalgia attachment. This is sourced-but-lopsided evidence, not an unverified problem statement. Council proceeds on that basis — treat every voice below as testifying against a *one-sided* Gate 1, not a *missing* one.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — the brief measures a sip, not a job

The blind taste test captures a narrow functional job ("which liquid tastes better in one mouthful, cold, no context") and the OKR treats winning that test as if it *is* the job the customer hires Coke to do. [A] on the key result names this gap directly: taste-test win → share recovery is an inference, not evidence.
Coke's actual job set is plural: functional (refreshment/taste), but also emotional (nostalgia, comfort) and social (ritual — "my brand," Saturday cookouts, generational identity). The brief has zero sourcing on the emotional/social jobs and flags this itself in "What's missing."
What existing solution gets fired when this ships? Possibly the original Coke itself gets fired by its own loyalists — the brief never asks whether the "competitor" being defended against is actually competing on the same job as the incumbent's most loyal base.

**Strongest objection:** JTBD would say the brief has confused the *metric* (single-sip preference) with the *job* (why people buy Coke). A brief that can win its own OKR and still lose the actual customer relationship has not found the job — this is Gate 2's core flaw, and it's under-flagged relative to how consequential it is.

**Verdict:** Weak.
**Why this strength:** The brief correctly tags the leap as an assumption [A], but doesn't escalate it — a single [A] tag under-weights a gap this load-bearing to the entire OKR.

### Service Blueprint — stage-adjusted: the blueprint isn't due yet, but the *flag* for one is

Scoring rule applied: at Gate 30 (concept/problem-framing stage) a full frontstage/backstage operational blueprint is not expected — dinging this brief for lacking one would be scoring the wrong stage. What *is* stage-appropriate at Gate 30 is naming that a service-design question exists and will need answering before build.
On that adjusted bar: full replacement (vs. parallel launch) collapses backstage to a single SKU/formulation with no rollback path — that's a real operational commitment being made at a concept-stage brief, and it's stated as a fait accompli ("discontinuing the original entirely") rather than flagged as an open service question.
Frontstage: the moment a loyal customer reaches for the product and finds it permanently gone is a designed (or undesigned) service moment. Nothing in Gate 3 names who owns that moment or what the customer-facing signal is.
Handoff: no support-process question is raised at all — not even as a "[?] to resolve before build," which is what stage-appropriate coverage would look like here.

**Strongest objection:** The stage-adjusted bar is low, and this brief still doesn't clear it — it's not that the blueprint is missing (expected at Gate 30), it's that the brief doesn't even register a blueprint will eventually be needed. A one-line flag would have cleared this; there isn't one.

**Verdict:** Weak.
**Why this strength:** Graded against the Gate-30 bar (flag the question, don't answer it) rather than the Gate-90 bar (answer it) — and even the lighter bar isn't met.

### Self-Determination Theory — autonomy removed, relatedness unmeasured, competence beside the point

Autonomy: full replacement is a forced-choice design — the loyal consumer's sense of control over their own preference is removed entirely, not offered as an option. This is the most direct SDT hit in the brief and it's a direct consequence of the Gate 3 decision ("discontinuing the original entirely rather than a parallel launch" [A]).
Relatedness: this is the dimension most exposed by a legacy consumer brand, and it's the one the brief admits it has no data on — the counter_metric ("loyalty/emotional attachment... must not be damaged") is marked [?] with no evidence source listed.
Competence isn't really in play here (this is a packaged good, not a system with a skill-feedback loop) — but note that's a sign the SDT frame itself is partially mismatched to this artifact type; still, autonomy and relatedness carry the weight and both fire.

**Strongest objection:** SDT would say the brief is optimizing the one dimension it can measure (taste preference, adjacent to competence-satisfaction) while remaining structurally blind to the dimension its own decision (full replacement, not parallel) most threatens (relatedness). That's not an oversight in the writing — it's an oversight built into the decision itself.

**Verdict:** Weak.
**Why this strength:** The brief self-flags the counter-metric as [?] — credit for naming it — but names it and then proceeds to the highest-autonomy-cost option (full replacement) anyway, with no mitigation proposed.

### Disruptive Innovation — a sustaining reaction wearing defense as its justification

This is not disruptive — it's a sustaining move: an incumbent reacting to a challenger that is winning on a single performance attribute (sweetness) among the *same* existing customer base, by matching the challenger's profile on that attribute. No new market, no unserved job, no simpler/cheaper tier being created.
Who loses position if this lands? Paradoxically, the incumbent's own loyal segment is the one put at risk — not the competitor. The brief frames this as "defending share" but the mechanism (full replacement) puts the defender's own moat (identity/nostalgia loyalty, per JTBD and SDT above) on the table to fight a fight defined entirely on the attacker's terms.
What does the incumbent have to defend? Brand identity equity that the taste-test metric cannot see and that this brief has no research on — the thing worth defending is exactly the thing not being measured.

**Strongest objection:** Disruptive Innovation would say the real strategic error isn't the reformulation — it's competing on the challenger's chosen metric at all, full-stop, rather than asking whether "sweeter single-sip preference" is a metric worth winning if winning it means vacating the incumbent's actual differentiator.

**Verdict:** Weak-to-Moderate.
**Why this strength:** Moderate credit because defending share is a legitimate sustaining objective in principle; weak because the chosen mechanism (total replacement, no parallel option tested) maximizes exposure of the untested variable rather than hedging it.

### Value Chain — the demand-side story may be covering a supply-side decision

Where this inserts: recipe/formulation layer — bottler syrup contracts, manufacturing changeover, retail shelf allocation. The brief evaluates the decision entirely on demand-side evidence (taste tests) and never surfaces the supply-side chokepoint that plausibly drove the "replace, don't parallel" call.
Margin effect: full replacement protects operational margin (one SKU, one contract renegotiation, one changeover) at the cost of forgoing a margin-additive option — a parallel-launch SKU could capture challenger-preferring customers without cannibalizing the loyal base, i.e., add revenue rather than gamble existing revenue.
Chokepoint control: retail shelf space and bottler contracts are the actual chokepoint here, not consumer taste. A 1-for-1 swap requires zero shelf renegotiation; a second SKU requires fighting for shelf space against the same competitor this brief is trying to beat. [A] tag on "replace entirely" may be a chokepoint-economics decision dressed as a consumer-preference decision.

**Strongest objection:** Value Chain would say this brief never asks who actually made the replace-vs-parallel call and why — if it was retail/bottler economics rather than the taste data, the entire Gate 1–3 problem framing is answering the wrong question convincingly.

**Verdict:** Moderate.
**Why this strength:** Credit for the brief's own [A]/[?] tags correctly flagging margin and evidence risk; held to Moderate rather than Strong because the actual chokepoint economics (bottler contracts, shelf negotiation) that would explain *why* full replacement was chosen are never named as a hypothesis, let alone tested.

---

> Disruptive Innovation and Value Chain diverge on what kind of decision this actually is — Disruptive Innovation reads "replace entirely" as a strategic error (fighting the challenger's metric, abandoning the incumbent's moat); Value Chain reads it as a possibly *rational* chokepoint-economics move (one-SKU changeover, no shelf fight) that's been mislabeled as a taste-preference decision. Whether this was a bad consumer bet or a defensible supply-chain bet dressed as one — that's a decision for the brief owner, not something the council resolves here.

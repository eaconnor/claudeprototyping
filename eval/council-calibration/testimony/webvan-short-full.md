# Council Testimony — Webvan Brief
**Brief:** `webvan-short.brief.md` — single line: "Webvan — online grocery delivery via company-built automated warehouses, expanding to many cities at once."
**Gate 1: UNVERIFIED.** No sourced customer problem, no data, no [R]/[D]/[A]/[?] tags — this is a one-sentence company description, not a research-backed brief. Council voices below are testifying against a historical case pattern-matched from training knowledge, not against sourced evidence in this brief. Treat as commentary until Gate 1 is filled.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen / Ulwick) — No job named, only a solution
- The brief describes infrastructure ("automated warehouses," "expanding to many cities at once") but never states the customer job. Functional job (avoid the trip), emotional job (reclaim time), or social job (status of never shopping in person) are all plausible and the brief distinguishes none of them.
- No "fired solution" is named. Does this displace in-store shopping, existing local delivery boys, or nothing — because the job wasn't urgent enough at scale to fire anything? Brief is silent.
- Solution-first framing (start with the warehouse, not the customer) is itself a JTBD red flag — the brief architecture mirrors "build capacity, assume demand."

**Strongest objection:** JTBD has nothing to grab onto here. A brief this thin isn't under-specified JTBD, it's absent JTBD — there is no stated job to test the solution against.

---

### Service Blueprint (Shostack / Bitner) — All backstage, no frontstage
- "Automated warehouses" is 100% backstage capital investment. The brief contains zero description of the frontstage experience: ordering interface, delivery windows, substitution handling, driver interaction.
- "Expanding to many cities at once" implies backstage build-out (warehouse automation, logistics, staffing) replicated simultaneously across markets with no mention of piloting or sequencing — no evidence any single-market blueprint was proven before scaling.
- Support processes for failure states — spoiled perishables, missed windows, refunds — aren't addressed. In a physical-goods, cold-chain business this is not a minor omission.

**Strongest objection:** A blueprint can't be evaluated when half of it (frontstage, support ops) doesn't exist in the brief. The one half that does exist (backstage automation) is being scaled to many cities before it's validated in one.

---

### Self-Determination Theory (Deci / Ryan) — Testifying against a vacuum
- Autonomy: centralized, company-built warehouses with simultaneous multi-city rollout signal a command-and-control operating model. Whether the customer retains control over substitutions, timing, or order changes is unaddressed.
- Competence: nothing in the brief describes the ordering experience, so whether the product makes a first-time user feel capable is unknowable.
- Relatedness: grocery shopping carries a social/community component (local grocer, neighborhood store). A model built entirely around remote automated warehouses may sever that relatedness — the brief doesn't acknowledge this tradeoff exists.

**Strongest objection:** SDT requires assessing lived interaction with a product. This brief provides no interaction detail at all — this voice is testifying on a design that hasn't been described, only its back-end infrastructure.

---

### Disruptive Innovation (Christensen) — Scale-first is the opposite of the disruption playbook
- "Expanding to many cities at once" is a scale bet, not a disruption test. Christensen's model calls for entering via an overlooked or underserved segment, proving unit economics, then scaling — this brief inverts that sequence.
- This reads as a sustaining play aimed at existing mainstream grocery customers (better convenience, same job, same customer base) rather than a disruptive entry from below serving nonconsumers with something simpler and cheaper. [CS: HIGH — historically Webvan targeted mainstream urban grocery shoppers, not an underserved segment]
- Incumbents (established grocery chains) already hold local trust, supplier relationships, and store networks. Webvan is building parallel capital-intensive infrastructure to attack a business incumbents already run at thin margin — a costly frontal assault, not a flanking move.

**Strongest objection:** Multi-city simultaneous expansion before proving unit economics in a single market is the single riskiest structural decision named in the brief — and it is the specific, well-documented mechanism of this company's real-world collapse. [CS: HIGH]

---

### Value Chain (Porter) — Capital intensity is the chokepoint, and the brief has no cost data
- Webvan's model vertically integrates the warehouse/fulfillment layer rather than partnering with existing grocery distribution — disintermediating traditional retail. That's a real value-chain insertion point, but the brief gives no data on whether this insertion is a margin play or a cost sink.
- Grocery retail is a famously low-margin business (net margins commonly cited around 1–3%). [CS: HIGH] Automating warehouses is a massive fixed-cost bet that automation-driven margin gains will outpace enormous infrastructure spend — the brief states the infrastructure choice but offers zero unit economics to evaluate the bet.
- The brief doesn't say who controls supplier relationships or last-mile delivery — Webvan controls warehouse automation, but the chokepoints of sourcing and fleet logistics are unaddressed.

**Strongest objection:** Without margin or cost data, Value Chain can't determine whether this insertion adds margin or destroys it. The capital intensity implied by "many cities at once" suggests the real chokepoint is access to capital itself — a risk this framework flags but the brief never names.

---

> **Named divergence:** Value Chain and Disruptive Innovation diverge on the same fact — the automated warehouses. Value Chain treats vertical integration into fulfillment as a potential long-term margin-protecting moat (control the chokepoint). Disruptive Innovation treats the same capital-intensive infrastructure, deployed simultaneously across many cities, as the fatal cost structure that outruns unproven unit economics. Same asset, opposite read — this is a decision about sequencing and risk tolerance, not a gap in either framework. Route it to the brief owner.

> **Named divergence:** JTBD holds that no customer job is identified in this brief at all — the solution precedes the job. Disruptive Innovation, by contrast, implicitly assumes the job is known (convenience for existing mainstream grocery shoppers) in order to classify this as a sustaining rather than disruptive play. One voice says the job is unstated; the other proceeds as if it's obvious. That's a real tension in how much this brief lets any framework assume.

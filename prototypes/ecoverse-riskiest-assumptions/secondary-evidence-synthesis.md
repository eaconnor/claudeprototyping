# What the secondary research actually says

**Elizabeth Connor** · 24 September 2026 · Internal working document

Twenty-seven scout dossiers, roughly 5,000 lines, run on 23 September. This is the read across all of them: what we set out to learn, what came back, and how much weight each answer can carry. Sources are linked below the table. Where the answer is "nobody knows," that is written down as an answer rather than left as a gap, because knowing a number doesn't exist saves the next person a week looking for it.

## First, back to the six

The deck named six risks on 09 September and set a test against each. That is where this started and it is what people have in their heads, so here is what the secondary round did to each one, in the deck's own order and using the deck's own wording.

| # | Risk | The assumption, as the deck wrote it | What the secondary did to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Value** — *Combined view creates value* | Connecting categories surfaces real, unrealized value | **Backed the deck's own doubt, not the assumption.** The deck worried that "the market still rewards products that are very strong at one clear job" and that bundling could hurt us. Interviewees say they arrive shopping for one problem, and the growth rates agree: asset discovery is the fastest-growing part of endpoint security at 12.8%, patching about 8.3%, and vulnerability management the slowest at 6.38%. We had been leading with the slow one. The one thing that would settle it — whether buyers set out to buy a point solution or a suite — **has never been published by anyone.** Not Gartner, not Forrester, not G2. Secondary cannot answer this and the mocked-view interview is still the only instrument that can. |
| 2 | **Demand** — *Pull without cannibalization* | This product vision will easily tie to business outcomes | **Found a trigger, and it isn't the one the deck was worried about.** The deck said "we think we can make an efficiency gain, but we don't know if that's enough of a draw." It isn't — nobody buys less effort, and there is no evidence that time-saving or AI is a switch trigger. But compliance is the top security investment driver at 70%, first of nine, and it is someone else's deadline rather than our efficiency claim. That moves the assumption from "efficiency will pull" to "a legal duty might." Two things still missing: the word *easily* should go, and **no source anywhere ties a compliance deadline to a rise in purchasing around that date.** |
| 3 | **Build** — *We can build it reliably* | We can build an AI that does this reliably | **Unchanged, and now externally grounded with a number to design against.** The deck's read — "correlating signals into judgment is harder than anything we have proved so far" — turns out to be right and measurable. On ITBench, agents resolved 13.8% of site-reliability scenarios and 25.2% of compliance ones; on the independent re-run every frontier model is under 50%; Microsoft's own AIOpsLab best is 59.32%. Separately, a synthesis of 20 studies puts the usefulness crossover at about **0.70 reliability** — below that, unreliable automation is worse than none. That is the acceptance threshold the deck's test was missing. |
| 4 | **Trust** — *Technicians will trust it* | The AI will be reliable enough for people to trust it out of the gate | **The deck's instinct was right and the deck's test needs a second instrument.** "Full autonomy is probably the wrong target; guided action with human in the loop is likely necessary" is confirmed by the market: Microsoft shipped its remediation agent read-only, Tanium requires sign-off. But two findings complicate the plan to ship the toggle and watch the 2.5/5 move. A field study of over 25 million real confirmation prompts found click-through from 10% up to 70.2%, so shipping a gate is not the same as earning trust. And self-reported trust has been shown to move independently of actual reliance — the 2.5/5 going up would not prove adoption, and it staying put would not prove failure. Also worth dropping "out of the gate"; it's the wrong bar. **And the two things the test names as the fix — approval gates and completion states — are split: gates have measured evidence, completion confirmation and audit trails have none at all.** |
| 5 | **Commercial** — *The need is payable* | We have a real, unmet business need that translates into commercial value | **Right on all three clauses, and now we can be specific about each.** Need: credible, and evidenced — patching is the hardest NIS2 requirement at 50%, 63% of organisations are a month or more late, only 34% of UK firms have a 14-day policy. Pricing: worse than the deck assumed. The comparison price isn't $8 a user, it's zero, because Intune arrives inside a bundle already bought for email and from 1 July 2026 that bundle absorbed more, and Action1 is free to 200 endpoints. **Cyber-insurance savings: still unvalidated, and now known to be contradictory** — ENISA ranks insurance last of nine drivers at 5%, while a DSIT study found 65% of UK SMEs had to meet security requirements to get cover, half of them spending £5,000 to £25,000. **Incident-cost savings: no independent controlled study of MTTR reduction exists** — the "40 to 60%" figures circulating are vendor marketing with no baseline or method. And there is still no willingness-to-pay study for this category anywhere. |
| 6 | **Moat** — *The advantage is a moat* | This product's advantage cannot be copied easily | **Overtaken by events, and the deck's hedge was the accurate half.** "Ownership may not be enough" was right. NinjaOne announced its Intune integration on 18 November 2025 and Action1 announced an equivalent at Microsoft Ignite the same month — no partnership, well inside twelve months. They did it differently, which the test wouldn't have caught: NinjaOne keeps control and treats Intune as a data feed, Action1 sits inside Intune and extends it. **Two threats, not one.** Retire the question as written and ask instead what they can't copy, and how long the lead lasts. |

### The vendor worth copying

**Tanium**, and specifically the governance mechanism rather than the product.

Everyone else in the set either claims autonomy without publishing how it's controlled, or doesn't act at all. Microsoft's Vulnerability Remediation Agent is read-only by design — the agentic identity gets Read Only Operator — so there's nothing to govern, and its own documentation admits there's no way to stop or pause it once started. Tanium claims autonomous action and then publishes the controls that make it safe:

- **Confidence scores** generated per deployment action from installation success rates *and* post-deployment performance baselines — CPU, memory, application crashes. Not a static risk rating. A measured read of whether this action, on this estate, tends to work.
- **Ring deployment** with configurable entry and exit criteria per ring, cited as 1% → 15% → 40% → all. Their own phrasing: start small and scale quickly when results meet confidence thresholds.
- **Sign-off required on every action.**

Why this is the thing to copy rather than admire: it is the mechanism that makes supervised automation shippable at *today's* reliability. The literature says below about 0.70 reliability automation is worse than nothing, and the benchmarks say nobody is near that on multi-signal work. Confidence scoring plus rings is how you ship useful automation anyway — you don't need the model to be right everywhere, you need the system to know where it has been right before and to expand only there. It also generates exactly the artefact our best play sells: a per-machine, dated record of what was attempted and what actually completed.

Two caveats. Tanium is an enterprise product and this is an enterprise design — ring criteria a 150-person company won't configure. And all of the above comes from Tanium's own solution material, so it is a description of a design, not independent evidence that the design works. Neither of those is a reason not to copy the pattern.

One other thing worth copying, and it's a pricing model rather than a product: **Atera charges per technician with unlimited endpoints at a flat fee**, and Gartner calls that out as unique and a strength. It changes the economics rather than the volume, it needs our own cost base rather than anyone's research, and it sidesteps the per-endpoint race to zero entirely. It's a decision, not a build.

### What this does to the deck's "what we need"

The test slide asked for owners and dates against tests 1, 2 and 6, on the grounds that 3 and 4 already had work in flight. That reading has changed in three places.

- **Test 6 doesn't need an owner, it needs a new question.** It has been answered, and the answer is no.
- **Test 5 is blocked in a way the slide didn't flag.** It says to apply the Cove and Adlumin attach-and-expand data to "Ecoverse's actual price points." There are no Ecoverse price points. And when they exist, the test has to run against a free alternative rather than a list price.
- **Test 2's first step is smaller than it looks.** Before commissioning a pricing study, someone needs to find out whether win/loss data exists at all. That's a question, not a project.

So the three that need owners and dates are now **1, 2 and 5**. Tests 3 and 4 can still start, with the amendments above — 0.70 as the reliability target for 3, and a behavioural measure alongside the 2.5/5 for 4.

Four risks have also been running without being written down: who this is actually for, whether onboarding can carry a self-serve trial, which unit the target is expressed in, and whether we can reach and convince this buyer at all. The last is the weakness Gartner names about us and it doesn't appear on either slide. Those are numbered 7 to 10 in the working document.

## Supporting evidence

### The problem, and who says so

The strongest sources in the whole corpus are the two government ones, because they have no product to sell.

- **ENISA, [NIS Investments 2025](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/nis-investments-2025)** ([main report PDF](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-02/NIS%20Investments%202025%20-%20Main%20report.pdf)) — 1,080 European organisations in NIS2-covered sectors. Compliance is the top investment driver at 70% against eight other options. Vulnerability and patch management is the hardest NIS2 requirement at 50%. On latency: 8% patch within a week, 31% within a month, 32% within three months, 28% take longer than three months. The trend is worth reading twice — in the 2022 edition 48% took one to six months; today 63% take a month or more. Security spend sits at a median 9% of IT budget, and security staff are down to 10.6% of IT headcount, the lowest ENISA has recorded. Their own words for where the money is going instead: technology and outsourcing.
- **DSIT, [Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026)** — 2,112 UK businesses, published 30 April 2026. Only 34% have a policy to apply security updates within 14 days, and DSIT lists it among the less commonly deployed controls. Breach rates rise sharply with size: 42% micro, 46% small, 65% medium, 69% large.
- **NCSC, [Cyber Essentials requirements v3.2](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/cyber-essentials-requirements-for-it-infrastructure-v3-2.pdf)** — the 14-day rule for critical patches, in force since 28 April 2025. This is the hard deadline the play below rests on, and it is a UK scheme.

One caveat to carry: ENISA notes its answer options were all drawn from NIS2 requirements, so this was a closed list rather than free response. It tells us which requirement is hardest among those requirements. It doesn't tell us what people would have said unprompted.

### The money

- **G2, [State of Software Buying 2026](https://www.g2digitalmarkets.com/hubfs/G2DM/PDFs/g2dm-state-of-software-buying-in-2026-report.pdf)** — 3,385 buyers across 11 countries, fielded August 2025, with the methodology printed on page 22. Security details 58%, value for money 51%, clear feature information 51%, ease of use 48%, pricing 47%. Vendors get dropped from shortlists for security or compliance gaps 55% of the time, unfavourable contract terms 52%, poor trial experience 50%. Buyers expect to prove a return in 8.75 months, and 50% call a trial the decisive factor against 35% for a sales presentation. Two limits, both real: G2 makes money from vendor visibility on review sites, and this is business software in general rather than endpoint management. It is the best published proxy and it is a proxy.
- **Microsoft, [2026 M365 packaging and pricing updates](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/licensing/news/2026-m365-packaging-pricing-updates)** and the [Intune pricing page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/microsoft-intune-pricing) — Microsoft 365 E3 went from $36 to $39 and E5 from $57 to $60 on 1 July 2026, and in exchange Intune Plan 2, Remote Help, Advanced Analytics, Privilege Management, Cloud PKI and Application Management moved into E3 and E5 at no extra cost. Intune Plan 1 standalone is $8 a user and the Suite is $10, but almost nobody pays that: Microsoft's own licensing documentation says most organisations get Intune inside a Microsoft 365 bundle rather than buying it directly. That is why the comparison price is zero. One asymmetry worth remembering in any per-endpoint argument — Intune is licensed per user, not per device.
- **Competitor pricing** — [Action1 is free to 200 endpoints permanently](https://www.action1.com/company-news/action1-expands-its-free-offering-to-200-endpoints/), not as a trial. [Automox](https://www.automox.com/pricing) publishes $1 per endpoint per month for patching only and quote-gates everything else. [NinjaOne](https://www.ninjaone.com/pricing/) publishes a range rather than a list — $1.50 at 10,000 endpoints rising to $3.75 at 50 or fewer — and says plainly why: it doesn't want to interfere with partner pricing. [ManageEngine](https://www.manageengine.com/products/desktop-central/pricing.html) is the only one with a full published list. Rapid7, ConnectWise, Kaseya and Ivanti publish nothing at all, and that absence is verified rather than a gap in our research.
- **Willingness to pay** — no study exists. Not for endpoint management, not for governed AI remediation, not for the bundle. The scout looked and came back with nothing, which confirms what the doc already said.

### The market size

The honest summary is that the public numbers can't carry a TAM argument. Published 2026 UEM market sizes run from $7.04B to $17.15B depending on who you ask, with CAGRs from 10.6% to 32.7%, and none of the report mills disclose whether "UEM" includes MDM, client management or endpoint security. Gartner's and IDC's own sizing is paywalled. What we can say with confidence is where the money concentrates: large enterprises are 71.6% of [endpoint management](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/unified-endpoint-management-market) revenue and 71.1% of [security and vulnerability management](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/security-and-vulnerability-management-market) revenue, and we have deliberately aimed below that.

On growth ordering, which inverts how we've been pitching: asset discovery is the [fastest-growing part of endpoint security at 12.8%](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/TableOfContent/endpoint-security-market-29081235.html), patching sits [around 8.3%](https://www.imarcgroup.com/patch-management-market), and vulnerability management is the slowest at 6.38%.

### The competition

- **[NinjaOne's Intune integration](https://www.ninjaone.com/press/microsoft-intune-integration/)**, 18 November 2025, and **[Action1's](https://www.action1.com/company-news/action1-extends-microsoft-intune-with-unified-cross-platform-patching-and-risk-based-vulnerability-management-at-microsoft-ignite-2025/)** at Microsoft Ignite the same month. The test in the deck asked whether either could do this within twelve months using a partnership. They needed neither a partner nor twelve months.
- **[Microsoft's Vulnerability Remediation Agent](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/intune/agents/vulnerability-remediation-agent)** is the most useful single page in the corpus. It is in public preview. The agentic identity is granted Read Only Operator in Intune and a Security Reader equivalent in Defender, so the agent is not technically capable of changing anything — it collects data, prioritises by CVSS and exposure, writes step-by-step remediation instructions, and a human applies them. Microsoft's own documentation states that an admin must start it manually and that once started there is no option to stop or pause it. It also states that admins can see agent-reported data outside their assigned roles and scope, and that scope tags aren't supported in preview. Windows and Intune apps only, public cloud only.
- **Microsoft documents coexistence as a supported architecture** — [18 named third-party device compliance partners](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/intune/device-security/compliance/third-party-partners) can act as MDM authority while Intune consumes the compliance signal, and there's a self-service onboarding programme. The catch that cuts against us: the supported platforms are Android, iOS/iPadOS and macOS. Windows is not on the list, so for a Windows-heavy estate this particular door isn't open.
- **Tanium** publishes the governance mechanism rather than just claiming autonomy: confidence scores derived from installation success rates and post-deployment CPU, memory and crash baselines, plus ring deployment with configurable entry and exit criteria, cited as 1% to 15% to 40% to all.

### The technology

This is where the corpus is strongest and where it most contradicts the obvious answer.

- **[ITBench](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/jha25a.html)** (IBM Research and UIUC, 42 authors, peer-reviewed in PMLR) — 94 real-world IT automation scenarios. Agent resolution rates: 13.8% for site reliability, 25.2% for compliance and security operations, 0% for FinOps.
- **[ITBench-AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/itbench-aa-launch)** — an independent re-implementation by Artificial Analysis with IBM, published 27 May 2026, scoring average precision at full recall across 59 tasks. Every frontier model is under 50%. Two failure modes they name are worth knowing: models submit more entities than there are actual root causes, and longer investigations don't produce better accuracy. The leaderboard is live, so pull the current figure at write-time rather than quoting a number from a launch article.
- **[AIOpsLab](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06706)** (Microsoft Research) — 86 incident scenarios, best agent 59.32% overall and 54.55% on mitigation.
- **[Onnasch, Wickens, Li & Manzey 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720813501549)**, a meta-analysis of 18 experiments — higher automation improves routine performance and lowers workload, and degrades both situation awareness and performance when the automation fails.
- **[Wickens & Dixon 2007](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14639220500370105)**, 20 studies and 35 data points — the crossover is around 0.70 reliability. Below that, unreliable automation is worse than no automation. This is the only number in the corpus that works as a design threshold, and it comes from diagnostic automation in lab tasks rather than action-taking automation in enterprise IT.
- **[Akhawe & Felt 2013](https://devd.me/papers/alice-in-warningland.pdf)**, over 25 million real browser warning impressions — click-through ranged from about 10% on Firefox malware warnings to 70.2% on Chrome SSL warnings. The same control mechanism, implemented differently, either works or is ignored seven times out of ten.
- **[Bansal et al. 2021](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2021-AIExplanationsTeamPerformance-CHI.pdf)** — explanations did not uniformly improve team performance and in some conditions increased over-trust in wrong recommendations. Worth having ready, because "add explainability and trust follows" is what everyone in the room will assume.
- **[KPMG and University of Melbourne 2025](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/xx/pdf/2025/05/trust-attitudes-and-use-of-ai-global-report.pdf)**, over 48,000 people across 47 countries — 66% use AI with some regularity and only 46% are willing to trust it, and trust has fallen since 2022 while adoption rose. General population rather than IT administrators, so treat the transfer as an assumption. But it does undercut the idea that trust arrives on its own with exposure.

Two failure cases are worth keeping on hand because they are primary, government-recorded, and fourteen years apart. [CrowdStrike's Channel File 291](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf) on 19 July 2024 — a template expecting 21 input fields, code supplying 20, an out-of-bounds read, 8.5 million Windows machines, and [a CISA alert](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2024/07/19/widespread-it-outage-due-crowdstrike-update). And [McAfee DAT 5958](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2010/04/21/mcafee-dat-5958-issues) on 21 April 2010, which flagged svchost.exe as malware and put Windows XP machines into reboot loops. Same shape both times: a fast automatic content channel that went round staged rollout. The controls CrowdStrike committed to afterwards — staged deployment, customer control of the update channel, bounds checking — are a requirements list written by an incident rather than by a designer, and they predate agentic AI entirely.

### Sources we are not using, and why

Worth writing down so nobody re-finds them and gets excited.

- **Any endpoints-per-IT-admin figure.** None is published. [Computer Economics](https://www.computereconomics.com/it-staffing-ratios/) is paywalled and discloses no figures at all on its public page, and every free version traces back to it through aggregators that disagree by a factor of two. Two of the publishers of these ratios are NinjaOne and Atera — direct competitors selling products whose whole pitch is raising the ratio.
- **Any IT-spend-as-percentage-of-revenue figure** from the SEO aggregator pages. They attribute to paywalled Gartner and Deloitte reports and contradict each other by 2x on the same segment.
- **Third-party per-endpoint prices** for NinjaOne, ConnectWise, Kaseya, Ivanti or Qualys from MSP comparison blogs. No methodology, commercially motivated, mutually inconsistent.
- **The "40 to 60% MTTR reduction" claim** that appears across half a dozen vendor pages. No methodology, no baseline, no control, and the pages don't even use MTTR consistently. It is evidence that this is the category's expected talking point, which is genuinely useful competitive intelligence, and it is not evidence that MTTR falls.
- **Insurance underwriting requirements** stated as specific thresholds — "MFA at 98 to 100%", "EDR on over 95% of endpoints", "patch compliance above 90% within SLA". Every source offering those numbers is a security vendor or MSP. None is attributable to an insurer, a carrier filing, a broker report or a regulator.
- **Canalys market share** via [ChannelE2E](https://www.channele2e.com/news/big-3-rmm-psa-vendors-see-market-share-slip-canalys) — our 11.4% against NinjaOne's 9.4% is the most encouraging number we have and we still can't use it yet. The article blocks automated reading, the period looks like one quarter of 2024 rather than a year, the fifth vendor is reported two different ways, and the shares don't sum as described. Someone should open it by hand.

## The one play that survives

Eight options went on the board. Three won't work, three are right but too slow or blocked, and one is untested. This is the one the evidence actually supports.

**Sell the proof, not the patching.** The product closes a named compliance duty on its own, inside rules the customer sets, and hands them the evidence it was done — dated, per machine, exportable, good enough to put in front of an auditor, an insurer, or a customer running a vendor assessment.

### Why this one

**The duty is real, dated, and someone else wrote it.** We don't have to convince anyone the work matters. UK Cyber Essentials puts a 14-day deadline on critical patches and has done since April 2025. NIS2 makes patching a requirement across the EU, and when ENISA asked which requirement was hardest, vulnerability and patch management came first at 50%. We are not creating demand, we are attaching to an obligation that already exists.

**The buyer is already spending for exactly this reason.** 70% of organisations name regulatory compliance as the main driver of their security investment — first of nine, against 42% for proactive risk reduction. And when ENISA asked what they plan to do next, implementing or upgrading security tools came first at 47%. The budget line exists and it has our name on the category.

**The gap between the duty and reality is enormous, and it's widening.** 63% of organisations take a month or more to patch critical systems and 28% take over three months, against a 14-day requirement. In the UK only 34% have a 14-day policy at all. Two-thirds of vulnerabilities being exploited already have a patch available. So the duty is real, the failure to meet it is documented, and the consequence is measurable.

**It doesn't need the thing nobody can build.** This is the part I'd push hardest in the room. Everyone's instinct is to sell autonomous remediation, and the benchmarks say nobody is close: 13.8% on site-reliability scenarios, every frontier model under 50% on the independent re-run, 59.32% as Microsoft's own best. Meanwhile the research on whether more autonomy is even desirable says it degrades situation awareness and failure-state performance, and that below about 0.70 reliability you are worse off than with nothing. A play built on supervised action plus reliable proof is buildable at today's reliability. A play built on autonomy is not.

**The proof layer is genuinely unoccupied.** Microsoft's Vulnerability Remediation Agent is read-only by design — the identity is granted Read Only Operator — and tells you what to patch. Microsoft's own docs say there's no way to stop or pause it once started, and that admins can see its data outside their assigned scope. Tanium requires sign-off and publishes confidence scores and ring deployment. Both are governing the *action*. Neither is selling the *evidence*. Nobody in the set is.

**And price isn't the battleground, which matters because we would lose there.** We cannot beat zero. Intune is already paid for and Action1 is free to 200 endpoints. But price ranks last of five shortlisting factors at 47% while security detail ranks first at 58% — and security or compliance gaps eliminate 55% of vendors who make the shortlist. A play that leads on compliance evidence is competing on the axis buyers actually rank first, not the one where we're standing on the floor.

**The window is real and it's dated.** Gartner names our AI work as a strength and names NinjaOne's generative AI as limited and roadmap-dependent. NinjaOne raised at a five billion dollar valuation in February 2025 and will close that gap. The lead is genuine and it has an expiry.

### What it rests on, and where it could break

Four things, in order of how much they'd hurt.

**The compliance anchor is UK and EU, and the product is meant to be North American or global.** Cyber Essentials is a UK scheme. NIS2 is EU law. ENISA surveyed European organisations. Our best evidence is from the two places whose regulators publish and run surveys, and I've been treating that as a description of our market when it is a description of where the data is. The North American equivalent probably runs through frameworks, customer contracts and insurance rather than one statutory deadline — but that is my assumption and nobody has checked it. A scout is running on it now. Until it comes back, this play rests on a UK deadline.

**Nobody has shown that a deadline produces a purchase.** No source ties a compliance date to a rise in tool buying around that date. We can show the pressure is real and we cannot yet show it converts. This is the single cheapest thing to test with our own win/loss data, if any exists.

**Nobody has measured whether proof moves anyone.** This is the uncomfortable one and it goes right under the play. Governance frameworks require logging, so a buyer will ask for it in an RFP — but there is no study anywhere testing whether an audit trail or a completion confirmation changes trust or adoption. Approval gates have evidence. Accuracy has evidence, with a threshold. Rollback has one on-domain data point from a vendor that sells rollback. Audit and completion have nothing measured. We are proposing to sell the one trust condition with no evidence behind it. That doesn't make it wrong — it makes it the first thing to test, and it's testable cheaply with the concept test already planned.

**The insurance leg is contradictory.** ENISA puts insurance last of nine drivers at 5%. DSIT found 65% of UK SMEs had to meet security requirements to qualify for cover, and half of those spent £5,000 to £25,000. Those aren't the same question, so both can be true, but we can't lean on insurance as a second forcing function until someone reconciles them. Note too that the DSIT survey's list of controls implemented for insurance doesn't include patch cadence or asset inventory as options at all — so it can't tell us insurers require patching, and it can't tell us they don't.

### How we'd say it

> For internal IT teams of two to six people running a few hundred to about fifteen hundred similar machines, who have patching and inventory duties with legal deadlines and nobody spare to do them, Ecoverse closes a named compliance duty on its own within rules you set, and hands you the proof it was done. Unlike Intune plus a separate tool, we treat the duty as the job rather than the machine, and we prove it was met.

Two versions of the last clause, for two different fights. Against Microsoft: we treat the duty as the job. Against NinjaOne: we ship supervised automation now rather than promising it. The second has a shelf life, and that is the whole argument for moving.

### The first three tests

Cheapest first, and each one can change the answer.

1. **Put the two versions of the differentiator in front of people in the band and make them pick.** Then ask which they'd forward to a colleague, and why. If both land the same, neither is distinctive.
2. **Count the customer stories we could publish in this band within ninety days.** This is an internal audit, not a study, and it caps everything else — Gartner's criticism of us is that we have very few published references and no industry-specific sales motion. If the count is near zero, proof is the critical path and more product is not.
3. **Query the install base for endpoints per customer inside the band**, broken out by region and industry, and return the spread rather than the average. One day of work. It unblocks the sizing and the pricing test, and it's the number no publisher has.

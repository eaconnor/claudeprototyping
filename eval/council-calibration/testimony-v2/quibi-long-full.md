# Council Testimony — "Quick Bites" (Quibi) brief
**Brief:** `eval/council-calibration/briefs/quibi-long.brief.md` | **Gate:** 30 (draft/concept stage)

**Gate 1 status: unverified.** Problem statement is tagged `[A]` — asserted from "executive experience/thesis," not cited user research. Key result and counter-metric are both `[?]`. Evidence sources list is two items: the executive thesis and a funding number ($1.8B raised pre-launch `[D]`). Capital raised is not customer evidence. Voices below testify against an assumed problem, not a sourced one — treat every downstream observation as conditional on that gap.

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — a solution went looking for a job to retrofit

- The brief names a functional job ("fill in-between moments") but sources it from executive belief, not customer interviews or diary studies. `[A]` This is the classic JTBD failure mode: the format (7–10 min "chapters," mobile-only, premium) was decided first; the job was written to justify it, not discovered before it.
- Failure mode #2 in the brief names the correct competitor for the job — YouTube and podcasts already fill commute-gap viewing for free — but the brief does not explain what differentiated value justifies a *paid* switch. No emotional or social job is named at all; only a functional one, thinly.
- The "fired solution" test cuts against the brief: users would have to fire a free, already-adequate solution (YouTube) to hire an unproven paid one. JTBD requires the new hire to be meaningfully better at the job, not just differently produced.

**Strongest objection:** The job was never validated as underserved — it was assumed underserved because an incumbent format (mobile short clips, free) already exists and was simply declared insufficient. `[?]`

**Verdict: Weak.**
**Why this strength:** No demand-side evidence exists anywhere in the brief; the only cited datum ($1.8B `[D]`) is supply-side conviction, not job evidence. At this concept stage that absence is expected to be *named*, but it is not expected to be *this decisive* — the entire OKR key result rests on an unfilled `[?]`.

### Service Blueprint — the one operational decision made is the wrong one to lock in this early

- Stage-appropriate scoring: at Gate 30/draft, a fully mapped frontstage/backstage split is not expected yet — no penalty for missing support-process detail (content ops, moderation, licensing backstage) at this stage.
- However, Gate 3 already commits to an irreversible backstage/frontstage architecture decision — "mobile-only — no TV app, no web app, no casting" — before the blueprint questions that decision depends on (does the target occasion require multi-device presence?) have been tested. That's a premature lock, not an appropriately-deferred gap.
- The brief's own Gate 3 note (`[D]` — stay-at-home orders eliminating the commute occasion) is a backstage/frontstage mismatch made visible after the fact: the frontstage promise (fill commute gaps) had zero backstage flexibility (no other screen) to absorb the shock.
- No support process is named for what happens to the content library or subscription base if the primary occasion disappears — this is a real backstage gap even accounting for stage.

**Strongest objection:** This framework would not fault the brief for an unfinished blueprint at concept stage — it would fault the brief for treating "mobile-only" as a settled architectural fact rather than an open blueprint question, given how much downstream risk (Gate 3, failure mode #1) hangs on it.

**Verdict: Mixed — appropriately thin for stage, but the one commitment that exists is premature.**
**Why this strength:** Applying the stage-appropriate rule, absence of blueprint detail ≠ weakness here. The weakness is a specific, named, irreversible choice (device exclusivity) made with the same confidence as the untested job — that's a scoring distinction, not a blanket penalty.

### Self-Determination Theory — the product spends autonomy to buy a premium price

- Autonomy: mobile-only, no casting, no TV app is a unilateral constraint on where/how the user watches — a straight autonomy reduction relative to the free incumbent (YouTube casts anywhere) the brief itself names as the competitor to beat.
- Competence: the bounded "chapter" format could support a sense of mastery/completion (a finishable unit), but this is speculative — no research cited that this format increases perceived competence versus an equally short YouTube video. `[A]`
- Relatedness: short-form content occasions (commute, waiting) are frequently social — sharing, discussing, reacting. The brief is silent on any social or relational feature; relatedness is neither supported nor undermined, it's simply absent from consideration.
- Trust/agency model: N/A in the AI-specific sense (this is not an AI system) — flagging that this sub-question of the framework doesn't transfer to this brief rather than forcing it.

**Strongest objection:** The brief asks users to *pay* for a *reduction* in autonomy (fewer viewing contexts than the free alternative) with no compensating competence or relatedness gain that's been validated. That's an unusually direct SDT violation for a paid product to carry into launch.

**Verdict: Weak.**
**Why this strength:** The autonomy cost isn't a discovered constraint the team is stuck with — it's a chosen one (Gate 3 build decision), made without evidence that competence/relatedness gains offset it. `[?]`

### Disruptive Innovation — this is shaped like a sustaining bet wearing disruptive language

- The OKR language ("own the in-between moments occasion") reads as new-market disruption rhetoric, but the mechanics don't match: disruption typically wins on cost/simplicity against an incumbent's overshoot. Here the product is premium-priced, high-production-value, and mobile-only — the opposite of the low-end, "good enough" wedge disruption theory describes.
- $1.8B raised pre-launch `[D]` is a capital signature of a sustaining, high-conviction bet, not a disruptive one — disruptive entrants typically start resource-constrained and improve from below.
- Who has to defend? Nobody, structurally. YouTube's free tier and Netflix's long-form catalog aren't threatened by a narrower, pricier, single-device alternative — the brief names the incumbent solution (failure mode #2) but never explains what forces the incumbent to react.
- No asymmetric cost or motivation advantage is named anywhere in the brief — the standard disruption tell (incumbent doesn't want to chase the low end) doesn't apply because this isn't the low end.

**Strongest objection:** This framework would say the brief misidentifies its own shape — it's marketed and funded like disruption, but structured like an expensive sustaining play into a niche occasion nobody was shown to be underserving. The risk sits entirely on the entrant; that's backwards from disruption's usual risk allocation.

**Verdict: Weak — theory mismatch, not just execution risk.**
**Why this strength:** The capital structure (`[D]`, $1.8B) and the product structure (premium, high-production) are internally consistent with each other but inconsistent with the disruptive framing used in the OKR objective. That's a strategy-labeling error, independent of the Gate 1 evidence gap.

---

### Value Chain — no chokepoint captured, margin funded by capital instead of demand

- Insertion point: content production + direct-to-consumer distribution, bypassing cable/broadcast. But mobile-only distribution means full dependence on Apple/Google app-store chokepoints — the product controls no distribution chokepoint of its own.
- Margin effect: premium subscription price is meant to add margin, but "high production value" (Gate 2 `[A]`) implies high backstage cost with no validated willingness-to-pay — margin is asserted, not modeled. `[?]`
- Compare to incumbents: Netflix and YouTube both hold the actual chokepoint here — multi-device presence, i.e., wherever attention happens. By choosing mobile-only, this product voluntarily exits the chokepoint the incumbents occupy rather than attacking it.
- The $1.8B `[D]` is functioning as a substitute for value-chain leverage — capital is buying market entry in place of a structural cost or distribution advantage. That's a fragile margin position: it lasts only as long as the capital does, which the counter-metric (`[?]` — retention post-marketing-wave) implicitly worries about.

**Strongest objection:** The brief never identifies what chokepoint this product controls or attacks. Without one, "premium subscription" is a price decision, not a value-chain position — margin is being requested from the customer, not earned from the chain.

**Verdict: Weak.**
**Why this strength:** Every other voice's weakness compounds here: no sourced job (JTBD), a premature autonomy-reducing architecture choice (Service Blueprint/SDT), and a sustaining-not-disruptive shape (Disruptive Innovation) all land on the same value-chain conclusion — capital, not chokepoint control or validated demand, is currently the entire margin thesis.

---

> Disruptive Innovation and Value Chain converge rather than diverge here — both independently conclude the product holds no structural advantage and is substituting capital for one. Where they *would* diverge from Service Blueprint is on how much to penalize the brief for this at Gate 30: Service Blueprint explicitly discounts for stage; the other two do not, because the strategic mismatch they're naming isn't a stage-appropriate gap — it's a category error in the bet itself, present at any stage. Route that distinction to the brief owner rather than resolving it here.

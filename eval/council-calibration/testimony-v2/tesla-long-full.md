# Council Testimony — Roadster Brief (gate 30, `eval/council-calibration/briefs/tesla-long.brief.md`)

**Gate 1 status:** Sourced but mixed-confidence. The core problem claim — EV1 discontinuation, "golf cart" image — is tagged [D] (publicly documented). [CS: HIGH] The forward claim that drives the whole brief — "no car has proven EVs can be aspirational" — is tagged [A], an assumption, not evidence. Proceeding with council on a partially-sourced Gate 1; the [A]/[D] split itself is worth noting to the brief owner, not just the individual voices.

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done — the customer's job is never named, only the company's

The OKR states the company's job ("prove EVs can be desirable") but not the buyer's. A $100k Roadster buyer is hiring the car for something — status signaling, outperforming a gas sports car, being visibly early to a movement — and the brief never says which. [A]
The brief conflates two different jobs: the company's strategic job (prove a thesis to unlock capital for model two) and the customer's job (whatever gets a wealthy buyer to spend six figures). The halo-effect logic — desirability at the top transfers to demand at the bottom — is asserted, not evidenced. [A]
What gets fired: not stated. Is the Roadster firing a Porsche 911, a Ferrari, or nothing (new discretionary purchase)? That answer changes the entire competitive read and isn't in the brief.

**Strongest objection:** The brief's central sequencing bet (prove desirability → mass-market pivot) depends entirely on a customer-side motivation the brief never names or sources. Without it, "halo effect" is a founder-thesis label on an unexamined assumption. [CS: LOW — no customer evidence cited, only founder thesis [A]]

**Verdict [this lens only]:** Good
**Why this strength, not one notch over:** The halo/status-purchase mechanism is a known, real pattern (see also symbolic-consumption effects in hybrid vehicle adoption) — the idea itself is structurally sound. It doesn't reach Strong Good because the brief supplies zero evidence that *this* buyer's job is the one being assumed; that's a real gap in the idea's foundation, not just thin documentation.

### Service Blueprint — most backstage gaps are stage-normal; one isn't

Frontstage: direct sales, no dealer intermediary — customer transacts straight with the company. [A] Backstage: no owned manufacturing scale, chassis/components sourced from Lotus, a low-volume specialist. [D] At gate 30, an unbuilt or borrowed backstage is expected — that's not evidence against the idea, per the stage-appropriate rule.
One backstage item is structural rather than "not yet built": regulatory/dealer-franchise-law risk in direct-sales states, flagged in the brief's own "what's missing" as [?]. That's not an unbuilt process — it's a legal chokepoint that can block the frontstage transaction entirely in specific jurisdictions regardless of company maturity.
Warranty/service backstage is also unaddressed: sourcing the chassis from a third party (Lotus) means post-sale support depends on a supply relationship the company doesn't fully control — a support process the brief doesn't mention at all.

**Strongest objection:** The dealer-franchise-law risk is the one backstage gap this framework would score down, because it isn't a maturity gap that time and capital resolve — it's a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction legal barrier to the frontstage transaction itself, and the brief only flags it as unknown [?] rather than assessed.

**Verdict [this lens only]:** Neutral
**Why this strength, not one notch over:** Manufacturing scale and third-party chassis sourcing are normal-for-stage and don't pull the score down per the stage rule, which would otherwise put this at Good. It stays at Neutral rather than Bad because the franchise-law risk is state-specific and has been navigated by later direct-sellers — a real, unresolved risk, not a proven blocker.

### Self-Determination Theory — plausible stigma-reversal story, no lived-experience evidence

Competence: a fast, attractive EV directly rebuts the "golf cart" stigma tied to prior EVs [D] — owning one could support felt competence/status rather than the sense of having settled. [A]
Relatedness: buying into a low-volume, mission-branded product ("accelerate the world's transition to sustainable transport") offers early-adopter belonging — relatedness to a stated movement, explicit in the OKR objective.
Autonomy: direct sales removes the dealer as an intermediary, which for the customer could mean more control over the purchase itself (no haggling, no dealer markup opacity) — but this is inferred from the sales-channel choice, not described as an intentional UX decision in the brief.
Nothing in the brief describes actual ownership experience — range in practice, charging access, service turnaround with a third-party-sourced chassis. Laptop-style repurposed cells [A] raise an open question about reliability that would directly affect felt competence day-to-day, and it's unaddressed.

**Strongest objection:** SDT needs a lived experience to evaluate. This brief is a business/sequencing thesis, not a product-experience description — the framework is reasoning from brand narrative, not from anything the brief says about what it's actually like to own or drive the car.

**Verdict [this lens only]:** Neutral
**Why this strength, not one notch over:** The stigma-reversal logic for competence and relatedness is plausible and grounded in a real, documented prior failure [D], which keeps it off Bad. It doesn't reach Good because there is no ownership-experience data in the brief at all — the framework has nothing concrete to confirm autonomy or competence beyond the marketing thesis.

### Disruptive Innovation — this is sustaining innovation wearing disruptive language

By Christensen's own definition, disruption enters from below — simpler, cheaper, serving an underserved or new-market segment. A $100k sports car sold to buyers who could otherwise afford a Porsche or Ferrari is a sustaining play: better performance, better narrative, same wealthy customer base. [A] The brief's OKR language ("accelerate the world's transition to sustainable transport") describes a disruptive mission, but the product described in Gate 2 is not the disruptive vehicle — it's the funding mechanism for one that hasn't been built or evidenced yet. [A]
Who loses position if this lands: incumbent gas sports-car makers (Porsche/Ferrari-tier) lose exclusive claim to "performance requires gas," an image threat, not a market-share threat yet. Mass-market automakers — the actual disruption target implied by the mission — face zero pressure from this specific brief; that threat is deferred entirely to an unbuilt second model. [A]
What the incumbent has to defend: a narrative, not a customer base, at this stage. The real defensive posture disruption theory would predict (mass-market automakers ignoring a niche, over-engineered threat) can't even be assessed because the disruptive product isn't in this brief.

**Strongest objection:** This framework would say the brief mislabels itself. It is not evaluating a disruptive innovation — it's evaluating a sustaining, luxury-segment product that is explicitly deferring its disruptive claim to a future, unproven, unevidenced second model (the mass-market pivot is tagged [?] for capital burn and battery cost curve). Calling this "disruption" now is a category error the brief doesn't name.

> Disruptive Innovation and Jobs-to-Be-Done diverge here on how to read the mission language: JTBD treats the halo-effect sequencing as a plausible (if unevidenced) customer-psychology mechanism worth taking seriously; Disruptive Innovation treats the same sequencing as a category mismatch — sustaining innovation using disruptive branding, with the actual disruption entirely deferred and unbuilt. This is a decision about how much weight the mission narrative should carry against the theory it invokes, not a gap in either voice's reasoning. Route it to the brief owner.

**Verdict [this lens only]:** Bad
**Why this strength, not one notch over:** Not Strong Bad — the sequencing mechanism (fund a future disruptive product with a present sustaining one) is a real, coherent strategic move, just mislabeled as disruption in the brief's own framing. It's Bad rather than Neutral because the framework's core question — who does this attack from below — has no answer in this brief; the disruptive target is absent, not just underdeveloped.

### Value Chain (Porter) — the whole sequencing bet rests on a chokepoint the company doesn't control

Direct sales removes the dealer layer from the value chain entirely — a margin-protective move (no dealer markup split), though the margin gain sits on a low-volume base, so the absolute dollar effect is small even if the percentage effect is real. [A]
Repurposed laptop-style cells rather than purpose-built automotive cells is a cost-chain choice: cheaper than bespoke cell R&D, margin-protective in the near term, with quality/safety tradeoffs the brief itself only flags as assumed. [A]
Chassis/component sourcing from Lotus means the company doesn't yet own a meaningful segment of its own manufacturing value chain — it's renting capability rather than controlling it, standard for gate 30 but worth naming as a dependency.
The chokepoint that actually decides the outcome of this entire strategy — battery cell cost curve — sits outside the company's control and is named directly in the brief's own failure mode #2, tagged [?]. The brief does not say who controls that chokepoint (cell manufacturers) or whether the company has any lever over it at this stage.

**Strongest objection:** The entire multi-year sequencing bet (low-volume now, mass-market later) is gated by a value-chain chokepoint — battery cell cost — that the company does not control, and the brief's own tagging admits this is unknown, not mitigated. That's the single largest unaddressed risk in the document, and it sits one layer outside the company's own chain.

**Verdict [this lens only]:** Neutral
**Why this strength, not one notch over:** Direct-sales margin logic is sound and real, which keeps this off Bad. It can't reach Good because the long-term thesis depends on an external chokepoint with no stated hedge, lever, or even monitoring plan — that's a structural risk sitting at the center of the strategy, not a peripheral one.

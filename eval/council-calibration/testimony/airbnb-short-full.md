### Jobs-to-Be-Done (Christensen / Ulwick) — the brief names the wrong job

- The brief frames the job as purely functional: "substitute for sold-out hotels." That's a capacity-overflow job — real, but narrow. [A]
- The job Airbnb's own growth narrative points to is social/emotional — "live like a local," belonging, novelty — not stockout coverage. If the brief's framing is taken literally, the product being described (air mattress in a stranger's house) is a worse hotel, not a different job. [CS: LOW — this is the standard startup-mythology account, not independently verified against primary Airbnb sources in this brief]
- What gets fired when this gets hired: not just hotels — also couches, hostels, and "not traveling at all" (price-gated demand). The brief only names one competitor (hotels), which understates the job's actual competitive field.
- No customer voice, interview, or data point in the brief. Every JTBD claim above is inference from the sentence itself, not from evidence the brief supplies.

**Strongest objection:** The brief describes a feature (air mattress + spare room) and calls it a job (hotel substitute). JTBD can't validate a job from a product description — this brief has the causality backwards, and no amount of reframing fixes that without customer data.

### Service Blueprint (Shostack / Bitner) — the backstage is entirely absent

- Frontstage in the brief: guest browses, books, stays in a stranger's home. That's it — no mention of listing creation, pricing guidance, check-in, or dispute resolution.
- Backstage is not addressed at all: identity verification, payment escrow, insurance/liability, cleaning standards, safety incidents. For a model built on strangers occupying private homes, this is the highest-risk part of the system and the brief is silent on it. [A]
- No human/AI or human/platform handoff is named anywhere, because no support process is named anywhere. There is nothing here to critique as "visible" or "invisible" — it simply isn't in the brief.
- "Substitute for sold-out hotels" implies a comparison to hotel service standards (front desk, staff, liability coverage) without acknowledging the backstage infrastructure hotels already carry that this model would need to replace or waive.

**Strongest objection:** A one-line brief cannot support a service blueprint. Every backstage observation above is a gap-flag, not an analysis — there's no support process, staffing model, or failure-handling path to actually blueprint.

### Self-Determination Theory (Deci / Ryan) — relatedness is doing unexamined work

- Autonomy: hosts set price and rules for their own space; guests choose among listings. Brief implies high autonomy on both sides, but this is inferred, not stated. [A]
- Competence: first-time hosts turning a spare room into a listing face a real competence gap (pricing, hosting norms, safety prep) that the brief doesn't acknowledge exists.
- Relatedness: staying in "each other's homes" is the one feature that most differentiates this from a hotel — proximity to another person's life. The brief treats this as incidental ("substitute for sold-out hotels") when it may be the actual product.
- Trust model: nothing in the brief describes verification, reviews, or recourse — so there's no way to assess whether the trust architecture supports guest/host agency or just asks both parties to absorb risk on faith.

**Strongest objection:** SDT needs a described interaction to evaluate autonomy/competence/relatedness against. This brief describes an outcome (a booked stay) with no interaction design in it — the analysis above is theoretical extrapolation, not a read of what's actually specified.

### Disruptive Innovation (Christensen) — reads as classic low-end disruption, if the framing is accurate

- "Substitute for sold-out hotels" as stated is a sustaining framing (overflow demand for an existing category) — but the underlying mechanic (spare room, air mattress, non-professional host) is textbook low-end disruptive entry: cheaper, lower-quality-by-hotel-standards, ignored by incumbents who don't see individual homeowners as competitors.
- Who loses market position first: budget hotels and hostels, not luxury — the brief's own example (air mattress) sits at the bottom of the quality curve.
- What the incumbent has to defend: regulatory compliance hotels already carry (occupancy tax, safety code, ADA, insurance) that a "spare room" model can bypass entirely in its early phase — this is a regulatory-arbitrage advantage the brief doesn't name but the model depends on. [A]
- The brief's own "substitute for sold-out hotels" framing undersells the disruption story — it makes this sound like capacity relief for the incumbent, when the disruptive read is that it's an attack from below that incumbents structurally can't respond to (they can't unbundle their compliance costs to compete on price).

**Strongest objection:** Disruption theory needs a demand-side non-consumer or overshot-market signal to be more than a label. The brief gives one sentence and no market data — "disruptive" here is asserted by pattern-match to a known case study, not demonstrated from anything in this brief.

### Value Chain (Porter) — inserts at the supply node, but the brief never mentions the platform layer

- The brief describes only the guest-facing transaction (rent a room) — it never mentions the thing that actually creates value-chain insertion: a platform that aggregates discovery, trust, and payment across millions of individual hosts.
- If a platform exists, margin effect is structural: no real-estate capex, no staffing — asset-light supply at hotel-adjacent price points. But the brief as written describes person-to-person renting, not a platform business, so this margin claim is an inference about a company the brief doesn't actually name or describe. [A]
- Chokepoint, if a platform exists: search/discovery and the trust layer (reviews, ID, payment escrow) — whoever controls matching and trust captures take-rate from both host and guest sides. Again: not in the brief.
- As literally written, this brief describes a peer-to-peer transaction with no intermediary at all — which would mean no chokepoint, no take rate, no value-chain insertion beyond the two parties finding each other somehow (also unspecified — how do strangers find each other's spare rooms?).

**Strongest objection:** Value chain analysis requires an actor with a position in a chain. The brief supplies two individuals and a transaction — the platform, the chokepoint, and the margin story are entirely council-supplied, not brief-supplied.
